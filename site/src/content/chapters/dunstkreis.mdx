Look up on a clear day and the sky is blue, look west at the end of it and it is red, and the same air does both. The
light of the Sun, white above the atmosphere, is scattered by the air on its way down, blue far more than red, so blue
reaches you from every direction while the Sun itself, seen through the longest stretch of air at sunset, keeps what is
left. dunstkreis computes that sky: the light of the Sun scattered, absorbed and bent by a model of Earth's air, for any
Sun, any height from eye level to orbit, and any camera, in physical units, on the GPU. It follows Hillaire's technique
<Cite id="hillaire2020" /> on the atmosphere model of Bruneton and Neyret <Cite id="bruneton2008" />, which osgHimmel
used <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />, and adds the air's refraction, four wavelengths instead of three, and a sky that
holds from the ground to the orbit of the International Space Station. Clouds, the Moon and the stars are other
chapters' business; the sky here is clear, and empty.

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, WebGPU compute passes, no dependency on any other package, the Sun handed over as
a direction vector. Two small tables are computed once per atmosphere, one of the whole sky whenever the Sun or the
observer moves, in a quarter of a millisecond on a desktop GPU, and every frame after that costs one texture lookup per
pixel. It writes into a texture you own and records into a command encoder you own; the code example in [What to compute when](#what-to-compute-when) is
the whole setup. The same sky lights a scene, as a cube map and nine numbers, and
can be exported as files for a renderer that never runs dunstkreis at all.

The sky below is that pass, drawn into a canvas: drag to look around, scroll to zoom, and set the moment and the place
under it, which every figure of the chapter follows.

## Why the sky is blue

Sunlight that reaches the ground has crossed some 8 km worth of sea-level air, and about a tenth of it never arrives
as a beam: the molecules of the air scatter it aside, into every direction, and the sky is that sideways light. It is
blue because the molecules are far smaller than the light's wavelength, and for such particles the scattering grows with
the fourth power of the frequency, <Term of="Rayleigh scattering">Rayleigh scattering</Term>: the 440 nm of blue is
scattered nearly six times as strongly as the 680 nm of red. Violet is scattered more still, and the sky is not violet
only because the Sun sends less of it and the eye sees it poorly. The strength of it, the
<Term of="Scattering coefficient">scattering coefficient</Term>, β, is one of the model's few numbers: at sea level
3.3 × 10⁻² per kilometer for blue and 5.8 × 10⁻³ for red <Cite id="bruneton2008" />, falling off upward with the air's
density, by a factor of e every 8 km, its <Term of="Scale height">scale height</Term>.

Dust, haze and water droplets scatter too, by <Term of="Mie scattering">Mie scattering</Term>, which hardly depends
on the wavelength and throws most of the light forward, within a few degrees of its direction. That is the white glow
around the Sun, the pale horizon of a hazy day, and the model's second layer, hugging the ground with a scale height of
1.2 km. Its <Term of="Phase function">phase function</Term>, how much goes where, is set by one number, the
<Term of="Henyey-Greenstein g">asymmetry</Term>, g, 0.8 by default. And a third layer only absorbs: the ozone in the
stratosphere, a tent around 25 km, takes out the orange and yellow of the spectrum and leaves twilight its deep blue.
Without it, as in osgHimmel, twilight goes gray.

Turn away from the Sun just after it has set, and the sky above the opposite horizon splits in two: a dark blue-gray
band along it, the <Term of="Earth's shadow" />, and above that a band of pink, the <Term of="Belt of Venus" />. The
dark band is the planet's own shadow, cast onto the air: the air in it is lit no longer by the Sun, only by the sky,
and so by light that has scattered more than once. The pink above is air the Sun still reaches, by rays that have
crossed hundreds of kilometers along the horizon and lost their blue on the way, scattered back towards the observer.
As the Sun sinks the shadow rises, and some 6° below the horizon it has merged with the darkening sky. Nothing in
dunstkreis draws either: a ray that crosses the shadow simply finds the Sun's light hidden there, the multiple
scattering still lit. Set <Fig id="sky" /> to a clear evening, look east and watch them rise.


Every pixel of the sky is a sum, and the parts can be switched off one at a time. Rayleigh alone gives a blue sky with
a dark, violet horizon and no glow around the Sun. Mie adds the glow and whitens the horizon. Ozone takes the yellow
out of twilight. And the light scattered once, straight from the Sun into your eye, is only part of it: the rest has
bounced two, three, many times, and it is what lights the shadow side of the sky, Earth's shadow after sunset among
it. Bruneton computed each <Term of="Scattering order">order</Term> in turn and stopped after four.
Hillaire assumed that light scattered more than once has lost any preferred direction, which makes every further order
a fixed fraction of the one before and the whole infinite sum one table lookup <Cite id="hillaire2020" />, held in the
<Term of="Multi-scattering LUT">multiple scattering table</Term>. For Earth's air the two agree to a few percent, and at
twilight that table carries most of the sky.

## Bent light

Air slows light down a little, and denser air more: its refractive index exceeds 1 by 2.778 × 10⁻⁴ at the standard
atmosphere's 15 °C and 1013 hPa <Cite id="edlen1966" />, in proportion to its density. So a ray crossing the atmosphere
curves towards the denser air below it, and what reaches the eye from the horizon left the atmosphere a little lower.
Astronomers usually take this from a fit, like the one for the apparent altitude <Cite id="bennett1982" />, which
sternzeit uses too. George G. Bennett, a professor of surveying in Sydney who navigated yachts in the Sydney to Hobart
race <Cite id="kelly2019" />, made it for navigators who worked out their positions on programmable calculators. It
holds for an observer on the ground looking up, and nowhere else.

dunstkreis traces it instead, through the same air it scatters light in: the refractive index follows the density of the
Rayleigh layer, and every ray bends between the same samples that integrate its scattering, the view rays as well as the
sunlight reaching each point in the air. Along a bent ray in an atmosphere that is the same in every direction, n r
sin(z) stays constant, with r the distance from the planet's center and z the angle from the vertical. That alone
decides where the ground starts, which also lifts the visible horizon: from 10 km up, by 0.2°. The same model covers an
observer on the ground, one high up looking down, and one in orbit looking through the planet's rim, where a grazing ray
crosses the air twice and bends by more than a degree.

Near the ground the traced refraction lands within a few percent of Bennett's above 5°. At the horizon it is some 10%
larger: the model's air thins out with one scale height, the real air more slowly near the ground, where the
temperature falls with height. The tables store the result: the sky-view table, indexed by where the ray is seen, how
far it bent; the transmittance table, indexed by where the light comes from, how much of it arrives. So the sky is
looked up, and the Sun's disc flattens near the horizon and stays in view after it has geometrically set, without either
being a special case (<Fig id="refraction" />).

## Through the air, from the ground to orbit

The observer can stand anywhere from eye level up to the orbit of the International Space Station, 408 km. Rising, the
horizon sinks below the horizontal, by some 3° from an airliner's 10 km and 20° from the station, and the sky overhead
darkens as the air thins, until above 100 km the pass stops looking the sky up and raymarches every pixel from where its
ray enters the atmosphere: the planet's lit face, its thin glowing rim, and the line between day and night. The lower
bound is 1 m, eye level give or take, and it is a bound for a reason: exactly on the ground the horizon is level, every
downward ray ends at once, and the sky looks like nothing a person sees. Raise the height in <Fig id="sky" /> and
watch the horizon drop; at 100 km the sky is black at noon.


A mountain ridge behind another is paler, bluer and lower in contrast, and painters since Leonardo have used that to
paint depth: the air between you and the ridge scatters sky light into the line of sight and takes some of the ridge's
own light out. The same tables give it, the light scattered along a ray of a known length and what is left of what
started at its end, and it is next on dunstkreis' list.

## Tables instead of raymarching

Three tables carry the sky. Transmittance says how much light gets through the air along any ray, multiple scattering
how much light arrives after more than one bounce, and the sky view holds the whole sky as seen from where the observer
stands, rebuilt whenever the Sun or the observer moves. The sky pass then only looks up one texel per pixel.

<Fig id="lookup" /> follows one of those pixels back. Its view ray is marched from the eye in 30 steps, short ones first
where it goes up, since the air is densest right at the eye, and the haze in it within a kilometer or two. At each,
sunlight comes in from the side: how much of it made it through the air is a transmittance lookup, by the step's
altitude and the Sun's angle there, and the light that bounced more than once is a multiple scattering lookup, by the
same two. How much of all that still reaches the eye is summed up along the way, step by step. Sixty lookups end up in a
single texel of the sky view, and the sky pass reads just that one. Point at a texel below or in <Fig id="tables" />,
and all of them follow the same ray.

Every table's size and every sample count is a parameter, and the defaults were chosen by measuring: each table against
one with many more texels or samples, by the error it causes in the sky. The transmittance table at 256 by 64 moves the
sky by 0.04% on average and 0.6% at worst; the multiple scattering table at 128 by 64 by 1.2% on average with the Sun
12° down, where Hillaire's 32 by 32 is off by 14%, since twilight leans on that table's sun angles. The sky view is 128
by 256, taller than wide, because its rows, the angle from the horizon, need the texels and its columns, the azimuth
from the Sun, hardly do: Hillaire's 192 by 108 with as many texels is four times as far off. Thirty steps along each
ray, short ones first where the ray goes up and the air is densest, keep the sky within 0.4% on average, and the
worst of it is deep twilight, at 5%. Half the samples cost a tenth of a millisecond less per rebuild and start to show
at the Sun's cutoff on the horizon. The numbers and how they were measured are in the package's research notes.


The tables are 16-bit float textures, four channels, one per wavelength, which hold full precision over some 30 stops.
Two of them need help. The multiple scattering table falls by orders of magnitude across the planet's shadow, within a
single texel, and a linear filter between two such texels would leak the lit side into the shadow, making twilight
skies several times too bright; so the table holds the logarithm of its values, and the filter interpolates
geometrically, as an exponential falloff does. The sky view holds the luminance times a power of two that follows the
Sun, as [From light to pixels](#from-light-to-pixels) explains, so its brightest value sits near 2¹⁰ at any hour. And
every lookup aims at the centers of texels, not their corners: half a texel off and the sky shows a seam at the horizon
rather than an obvious error. Each table's mapping from angle and height to texel is exactly invertible, so the pass
that writes a table and the one that reads it agree to the bit.

## What to compute when

Nothing in the sky moves at a frame's pace. The Sun crosses its own diameter in about two minutes, a quarter of a
degree a minute; the air changes with the weather, over hours; only the camera turns every frame. dunstkreis is cut
along those lines, into four pieces that each have their own reason to run, and a renderer chooses how often each one
does. The choice is the whole art of using it: the same code renders a sky that is fixed for good and one that follows
a clock, at a cost from nothing per frame to a few tenths of a millisecond.

The two tables of the air, transmittance and multiple scattering, know only the atmosphere: the planet, the air's
density and its dust, the ozone, the refraction. They run once per atmosphere, in 0.6 ms on a desktop GPU, and never
again until the air itself changes. A renderer with one fixed atmosphere computes them at startup, or not at all: the
[generator](#make-your-own) exports them as files.

The sky view, the whole sky from where the observer stands, knows the Sun and the observer's height. It is rebuilt
whenever either moves, together with the light meter that reads it, in about a quarter of a millisecond, and the
rebuild is skipped when neither did. A sky fixed at one moment rebuilds it once. A sky following the clock rebuilds it
whenever the Sun has moved far enough to matter: every frame, if you like, since a quarter millisecond is cheap, or
every ten seconds, when the Sun has moved a tenth of its diameter. Refraction lives here and in the tables, never in
the per-pixel pass, so bent rays cost the same per frame as straight ones.

The sky pass runs every frame, one lookup per pixel into the sky view, 0.05 ms for a full HD frame, and it is where the
camera comes in. It is also where the [exposure and the tone curve](#from-light-to-pixels) are applied, or not: a
renderer with a pipeline of its own takes the exposed luminance, linear and unclamped, and tone maps the whole frame
with the sky in it. Lighting a scene adds a fourth piece behind the sky view: the cube map and its nine coefficients,
rebuilt when the sky view is, at the cost of a few sky passes, and sampled every frame after that.

Two renderers at the extremes: a skybox and a live sky. The skybox computes the tables and the sky view once, writes
the cube map, and keeps nothing of dunstkreis at runtime but a texture and nine numbers, tone mapped in whatever
pipeline draws the rest of the scene; its refraction, its exposure and its colors are the same as the live sky's,
since they were decided when the map was written. The live sky keeps the tables, rebuilds the sky view as the Sun
moves, and runs the sky pass every frame, exposed by its own meter if the renderer has no exposure. Anything in between
is a matter of how often `update` is called and whether a cube map is asked for (<Fig id="clocks" />).

<Clocks />

dunstkreis records into a command encoder you own, writing into a texture you own. It creates no device, no canvas and
no render pass of its own, so it slots in before whatever else your frame draws. Every further example builds on this
one.

**Feed it a sky.** Precompute the tables once per atmosphere, create a sky pass for your target, and hand it the Sun and
a camera. The Sun's direction is any unit vector in ENU: sternzeit computes it here, but dunstkreis does not depend on
it, and a fixed vector or your engine's own light does just as well.

```ts
import { createSkyPass, precomputeAtmosphere } from "@himmelszelt/dunstkreis";
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, sun } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Once per atmosphere: the transmittance and multiple scattering tables.
const luts = await precomputeAtmosphere(device);
// Into a canvas configured with rgba8unorm and STORAGE_BINDING usage.
const sky = await createSkyPass(device, { luts, format: "rgba8unorm", autoExposure: true });

// Whenever the moment, the place or the camera changes.
const time = fromDate(new Date());
const observer = { latitude: 52.39, longitude: 13.13, heightM: 10 };
sky.update({
    // Unit vectors in ENU: x east, y north, z up. True, not refracted.
    sunDirection: sun.direction(time, observer),
    sunAngularDiameter: sun.apparentAngularDiameter(julianEphemerisDay(time)),
    observerHeightM: observer.heightM,
    inverseViewProjection, // your camera's without translation, column-major; reversed-z works too
});
const encoder = device.createCommandEncoder();
sky.encode(encoder, context.getCurrentTexture());
device.queue.submit([encoder.finish()]);
```

`update` only rewrites a few numbers when just the camera moves. A new Sun or a new height also rebuilds the table of
the whole sky, which the pass then reads once per pixel.

## Four wavelengths

Scattering, absorption and the Sun's own light all change with the wavelength, so the sky is computed per wavelength,
not per color. A color on a screen is something else: its red, green, and blue each sum the whole spectrum, weighted by
how the eye responds to it <Cite id="wyman2013" />. Bruneton and Neyret computed three wavelengths, 680, 550, and
440 nm, and showed them as red, green, and blue <Cite id="bruneton2008" />, as real-time skies have mostly done since,
osgHimmel's included. Weighted by the eye instead, the same light comes out 12% off in hue on average, and a third at
sunset: none of the three samples sees the orange around 600 nm, where red peaks for the eye and ozone absorbs.
Bruneton's later implementation computes some fifteen wavelengths and converts them exactly <Cite id="bruneton2017" />,
at about five times the cost.

dunstkreis takes a middle road. It computes four wavelengths, one per channel of its tables, which have four anyway,
and turns them into red, green, and blue by a matrix fitted for it: by least squares, to 85 sky spectra built from the
model's own physics, sunlight through every depth of air and ozone, scattered by molecules or by haze, with the Sun
itself held white. Which four was a search: every set on 10 nm steps between 420 and 690 nm, 4,788 of them, each fitted
and then tested on 72 skies it was not fitted to. 630, 580, 510, and 440 nm came out best, 0.6% off on average and 1.7%
at worst, and the GPU does not notice: it computes on four components either way. We have not found this middle road
described before. The model choice in <Fig id="sky" /> shows the sky as it was colored before, Bruneton's three
wavelengths taken as red, green, and blue, and as osgHimmel computed it.

The four are a field of the model, and a model with other four gets its own matrix, fitted the same way; one default
set is what ships (<Fig id="wavelengths" />).

## From light to pixels

The sky comes out in physical units: luminance, in candela per square meter. The model's sun has the spectrum
Bruneton's tables use and the illuminance it has above the atmosphere, 128 000 lux: the solar constant of
1361 W/m² <Cite id="kopp2011" /> times about 94 lumens per watt for sunlight. That puts a clear midday sky at some
thousands of cd/m², the Sun's disc near 1.6 × 10⁹, and the sky at the end of twilight around a thousandth.

The sky-view table holds them in 16-bit floats, which keep full precision over some 30 stops, from 6 × 10⁻⁵ to 65 504.
The sky spans more than that in a day: its brightest value falls by 37 stops from noon to the Sun 30° down, and held in
cd/m², deep twilight would coarsen into steps that an exposure for the night magnifies into colored blocks. So the table
holds the luminance times a power of two that follows the Sun, which keeps its brightest value near 2¹⁰ at any hour,
and whatever reads it divides by the same.

Between those numbers and a pixel lie five steps, and every one of them is a choice a renderer makes: which three
numbers stand for the light, how bright it is shown, how its range is squeezed into the screen's, what becomes of a
color the screen cannot show, and how the result is written into eight bits. dunstkreis takes all five by default and
hands each one back on request, so the rest of this section walks them in order.

The light leaves the tables at four wavelengths, and a screen wants red, green and blue. The matrix from
[Four wavelengths](#four-wavelengths) does that, into linear sRGB, where a number is proportional to light and adding
two skies adds their light. Linear is the keyword: the physics works here and nowhere else, which is why neither the
tables nor the sky pass ever see a gamma curve or a perceptual space. The matrix is fitted, and a fitted matrix can give
a color the screen has no red for. The Sun at the horizon is one: its light has crossed thirty times the air that stands
above us, and what is left of it is redder than the reddest red of sRGB, so its true sRGB has a blue below zero, not by
a rounding error but by five percent of its brightness. Three wavelengths shown as red, green and blue cannot say so,
since each channel is a measured amount of light and never negative; four fitted ones can, and do. dunstkreis pulls
such a color back into the gamut by mixing in the gray of its own brightness until no channel is negative, which keeps
what it can of the hue. Left to a tone curve, that negative blue would be cut off at zero per channel, and the setting
Sun's disc split into a white upper and a yellow lower half at a hard line.

Then the exposure scales the light to the screen's white, the tone curve folds what is
brighter than white into it, and the last step writes the result for the display: through the sRGB transfer curve, the
"gamma" that spends more of the eight bits on dark values, where the eye tells them apart, and with a step of noise
against the bands a smooth twilight would otherwise fall into. Two displays want more, and both are the last step's
business alone: a float canvas with extended tone mapping shows values above white, up to its headroom, and a canvas in
Display P3 shows the saturated reds an sRGB screen gives up; what either does with a color still outside its range is
the gamut mapping at the end of the section.

Exposure is set in EV100, as on a camera and in engines like Frostbite and Unreal <Cite id="lagarde2014" />: the
luminance that just saturates is 1.2 × 2^EV100 cd/m². A sunny day is about EV 15, an overcast one 10, twilight 0,
moonlight −6. Since the sky is in cd/m², it takes the same exposure as any other scene built on this convention, and
looks the same under it.

Automatic exposure is an option, for a renderer without an exposure of its own, or to follow day into night. A light
meter on the GPU reads the sky-view table in directions spread evenly over the sky above the horizon, and takes the
geometric mean of their luminance, as a camera's averaging meter does: EV100 = log2(L · 100 / 12.5). It meters the
directions below the horizon apart and takes the brighter: on the ground that is the sky, high up the lit air below,
while the sky above turns black, and from space the lit planet, raymarched like the view. No direction counts more than
ten stops below the brightest, a camera's range, so the black sky and the night side do not drown out the day side. The
sky pass reads that straight from the meter's buffer. Metering the table rather than the image means the reading does
not change as the camera turns, and the Sun's disc, which is not in the table, does not darken everything. The meter
reads again whenever the table is rebuilt; the exposure follows at once, without the eye's slow adaptation. The day
meters around EV 14, a sunset around 12, and twilight follows down to 4, with the Sun 6° below the horizon, where it
stops: exposed like day, the night would look like day, only noisier. On top, a ramp over the day shapes it:
compensations at sun altitudes above the observer's horizon, which sinks as the observer rises, joined by a smooth curve
that never overshoots them <Cite id="fritsch1980" />. By default they keep the night and the minutes around sunrise and
sunset darker than metered, lift the blue hour a little so its blue shows, make the sunset a little brighter than the
sunrise, and leave noon as metered. Mornings and evenings may differ, though the model's air is the same for both (<Fig id="exposure-day" />).

The floor of EV 4 is for now. A camera exposes a landscape by full moon at about EV −3 and the Milky Way at about −6
to −7. Once the Moon lights the air and the stars are drawn, the floor drops and the night follows. Exposed that far
today, the sky would show only the Sun's last twilight, amplified beyond what the table can hold.

Metering needs the table on the GPU and a reading back, which not every renderer wants. So the meter can run once per
atmosphere instead, over sun altitudes from twilight to noon, the compensations added, and the result kept as a handful
of EV100 values at sun altitudes, joined by the same curve: a default exposure for that air, small enough to keep as
numbers in code. The default atmosphere's is to ship with dunstkreis. It knows only the Sun, not the observer's height,
and nothing of clouds or a Moon.


For a display, the pass then tone maps: exposed, compressed into the display's range, and encoded for sRGB, dithered
against banding. Khronos PBR Neutral <Cite id="khronos2024" /> keeps colors exactly as they are up to three quarters of
white and compresses only the brightest, so the sky's blue stays the blue the model computes. AgX
<Cite id="sobotka2023" />, in Wrensch's fit <Cite id="wrensch2023" />, fades bright colors towards white as film does:
the glow around the Sun and a sunset's brightest band turn white instead of clipping to a flat hue, but the daytime sky
comes out flatter and grayer. Narkowicz's fit of the ACES filmic curve <Cite id="narkowicz2016" />, which dunstkreis
used first, is the third: saturated, and it pushes bright hues towards yellow. The exposure follows a camera's
convention, which puts a metered average at about a tenth of white, where ACES expects it; Neutral and AgX expect the
scene's middle gray at 0.18, and are lifted by the difference. The tone curve choice in <Fig id="sky" /> switches every
figure between them. A renderer with its own HDR pipeline turns that off and gets the exposed luminance itself, linear
and unclamped, for a float target.


A display that shows more gets more, and only the last step changes. An rgba16float canvas with extended tone mapping
shows values above white up to the display's headroom, a few times SDR white on today's screens, and the Neutral
curve takes that headroom as its ceiling, so the Sun's glow and a bright horizon stay bright instead of compressed to
white. The HDR choice in <Fig id="sky" /> switches to it where the browser and the screen offer it. A canvas in
Display P3 would show the saturated reds an sRGB screen gives up, with the matrix fitted for P3 instead; whether enough
of a sunset leaves sRGB to be worth it is being measured. Either way a color still outside the display's range is cut
off per channel today, which shifts its hue; reducing its chroma at the same lightness and hue, as CSS does since
Color 4, keeps it, and is the next option of the tone map.

## Lighting a scene

The same sky can light a scene. The sky pass writes it into the six faces of a cube map, in cd/m² and without the sun's
disc, and a second pass projects that onto nine spherical harmonics coefficients, which give the irradiance for any
surface's orientation <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />. The disc stays out on purpose: at some two billion cd/m² it is far
brighter than a half float holds, and at half a degree smaller than a texel of all but the largest cube maps, so it
would be one blocky texel, smeared by the mip levels, and the coefficients would ring with it. Wherever the cube map is
shown, the disc is drawn over it instead, sharp at any resolution. The Sun lights the scene apart, as a light of its
own, with what the air leaves of it.

Why a cube map, rather than the atmosphere itself for every pixel and every surface? Because the sky hardly changes from
one frame to the next. The Sun moves by a quarter of a degree a minute, its own diameter in about two, and at 60 frames
per second that is thousands of frames each computing the same sky again. So the cube map is built once whenever the Sun
or the observer has moved, and every frame after that only samples it: one filtered fetch per direction, for the
background as for a reflection, and nine coefficients for all of the diffuse light. Lighting needs the whole sky anyway,
not only the part the camera sees. At 128 texels a face, a build computes 98,304 texels, a twentieth of the pixels of a
full HD frame; how long it takes shows above the figure.

A cube map's faces are six perspective views, and a perspective crowds the most directions into its middle: a texel at a
face's corner covers only a fifth of the solid angle of one at its center. So the sky is resolved five times as finely
towards the corners as in the middle of a face, and where two faces meet, the texel grid turns abruptly, which can show
in gradients as smooth as the sky's. Cubified, as osgHimmel's `CubeMappedHimmel` did it <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />,
the texels are placed by Nowell's mapping of a cube onto a sphere <Cite id="nowell2005" /> instead. The largest then
covers 1.31 times the solid angle of the smallest, a little more even than the equi-angular cube map YouTube streams
video in, at 1.41 <Cite id="brown2017" />. It costs a few instructions per lookup, and whoever samples the cube map has
to know: it looks up the point on the cube that maps to its direction, not the direction itself. Filtering across the
faces' edges and the mip levels work as before. The two figures below switch it together.

The cube map comes with its mip levels, each texel the mean of four in the level above, down to a single texel a face.
They cost a third more memory and a few cheap passes, and they are optional: the cube pass fills as many levels as the
texture was created with, so a `mipLevelCount` of 1 leaves them out. Two readers need them here. The projection onto the
coefficients takes 4,096 directions; from a face of 1,024 texels it would see only one texel in some 1,500 and miss
whatever lies between, so it reads the level whose texels are about as many as its directions. And a background showing
the cube map through a wide lens spans several texels with each pixel, so it reads the level where a texel is about a
pixel wide, instead of picking one texel of many and flickering as the view turns.

Nine numbers per color channel hold the sky's diffuse light, as a sound is held by a few of its lowest tones (<Fig id="harmonics" />): the first is the
sky's average brightness, the next three say how much brighter it is above than below and towards the Sun than away
from it, and the last five bend that into the shape of a lit horizon over a dark ground. A diffuse surface sees the
sky blurred over the whole hemisphere it faces, and nine terms of that blur keep more than 99% of the light it receives,
for any sky <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />. A shader turns them into the light on any surface with nine multiplies and
no texture lookup, which is what `createIrradiancePass` writes into a buffer, next to an irradiance cube map computed
from the same nine for shaders that prefer a lookup.

## Precise and approximate

The two implementations this chapter rests on make the same sky by different tables. Bruneton's holds the light
scattered towards an observer at any height, in any direction, for any Sun, in one four-dimensional table of 32 by 128
by 32 by 8 texels, computed once; aerial perspective between any two points comes from it for free, and the sky needs no
table of its own per Sun. Hillaire's holds two small tables of the air and rebuilds a table of the whole sky whenever the
Sun moves, in a quarter of a millisecond; it is what dunstkreis uses, fits in half a megabyte, and its multiple
scattering is an infinite sum under one assumption where Bruneton's is four orders exactly. For Earth's air the skies
differ by a few percent, mostly in twilight. Bruneton's table may come to dunstkreis as another way to fill the sky view,
and the two would then be switchable like sternzeit's precise and approximate variants.

## The people behind the numbers

Eric Bruneton and Fabrice Neyret, at INRIA in Grenoble, wrote the 2008 paper whose model of the air every number here
starts from <Cite id="bruneton2008" />, and Bruneton's later implementation, which computes fifteen wavelengths exactly,
is where the solar spectrum and the ozone cross-sections come from <Cite id="bruneton2017" />. Sébastien Hillaire, then
at Epic Games, replaced the four-dimensional table with the three small ones of this chapter, for a sky cheap enough to
rebuild every frame <Cite id="hillaire2020" />. Tomoyuki Nishita and his colleagues drew the first computed Earth seen
through its atmosphere in 1993 <Cite id="nishita1993" />, and the thickness of the shell is still theirs.

Bengt Edlén, a physicist at Lund, measured the refractive index of air that bends every ray here
<Cite id="edlen1966" />, and George G. Bennett's fit for navigators <Cite id="bennett1982" /> is what the traced
refraction is tested against. Ravi Ramamoorthi and Pat Hanrahan, at Stanford, showed in 2001 that nine numbers hold
the diffuse light of any environment <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />. Greg Kopp and Judith Lean measured the Sun's
constant anew in 2011, lower than before, 1361 W/m² <Cite id="kopp2011" />, which sets the sky's brightness, and
Sébastien Lagarde and Charles de Rousiers wrote down for Frostbite the exposure convention the sky is shown with
<Cite id="lagarde2014" />. The tone curves are Khronos' <Cite id="khronos2024" />, Troy Sobotka's AgX
<Cite id="sobotka2023" /> in Benjamin Wrensch's fit <Cite id="wrensch2023" />, and Krzysztof Narkowicz's fit of the
ACES curve <Cite id="narkowicz2016" />.
