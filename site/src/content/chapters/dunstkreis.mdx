## Pick a moment and a place

<Controls open />

dunstkreis knows nothing of dates: it takes the Sun as a plain direction, here from sternzeit's `sun.direction`. That is
already where the Sun shows rather than where it is, its light 8 minutes 20 seconds underway, as
[sternzeit explains](../sternzeit/#the-sun). It is the true direction nonetheless, not yet bent by the air: dunstkreis
traces the refraction itself. How to hand it over is the first of the [code examples](#code-examples).

## Why is the sunset red?

Turn away from the sun just after it has set, and the sky above the opposite horizon splits in two: a dark blue-gray
band along it, the <Term of="Earth's shadow" />, and above that a band of pink, the <Term of="Belt of Venus" />. The
dark band is the planet's own shadow, cast onto the air: the air in it is lit no longer by the sun, only by the sky,
and so by light that has scattered more than once. The pink above is air the sun still reaches, by rays that have
crossed hundreds of kilometers along the horizon and lost their blue on the way, scattered back towards the observer.
As the sun sinks the shadow rises, and some 6° below the horizon it has merged with the darkening sky. Nothing in
dunstkreis draws either: a ray that crosses the shadow simply finds the sun's light hidden there, the multiple
scattering still lit. Set <Fig id="sky" /> to a clear evening, look east and watch them rise.

## Four wavelengths

Scattering, absorption and the Sun's own light all change with the wavelength, so the sky is computed per wavelength,
not per color. A color on a screen is something else: its red, green, and blue each sum the whole spectrum, weighted by
how the eye responds to it <Cite id="wyman2013" />. Bruneton and Neyret computed three wavelengths, 680, 550, and 440 nm,
and showed them as red, green, and blue <Cite id="bruneton2008" />, as real-time skies have mostly done since, osgHimmel's
included. Weighted by the eye instead, the same light comes out 12% off in hue on average, and a third at sunset: none
of the three samples sees the orange around 600 nm, where red peaks for the eye and ozone absorbs. Bruneton's later implementation
computes some fifteen wavelengths and converts them exactly <Cite id="bruneton2017" />, at about five times the cost.

dunstkreis takes a middle road. It computes four wavelengths, one per channel of its tables, which have four anyway,
and turns them into red, green, and blue by a matrix fitted for it: by least squares, to 85 sky spectra built from the
model's own physics, sunlight through every depth of air and ozone, scattered by molecules or by haze, with the Sun
itself held white. Which four was a search: every set on 10 nm steps between 420 and 690 nm, 4,788 of them, each fitted
and then tested on 72 skies it was not fitted to. 630, 580, 510, and 440 nm came out best, 0.6% off on average and 1.7%
at worst, and the GPU does not notice: it computes on four components either way. We have not found this middle road
described before. The model choice in <Fig id="sky" /> shows the sky as it was colored before, Bruneton's three
wavelengths taken as red, green, and blue, and as osgHimmel computed it.

## Bent light

Air slows light down a little, and denser air more: its refractive index exceeds 1 by 2.778 × 10⁻⁴ at the standard
atmosphere's 15 °C and 1013 hPa <Cite id="edlen1966" />, in proportion to its density. So a ray crossing the atmosphere
curves towards the denser air below it, and what reaches the eye from the horizon left the atmosphere a little lower.
Astronomers usually take this from a fit, like Bennett's for the apparent altitude <Cite id="bennett1982" />, which
sternzeit uses too. It holds for an observer on the ground looking up, and nowhere else.

dunstkreis traces it instead, through the same air it scatters light in: the refractive index follows the density of the
Rayleigh layer, and every ray bends between the same samples that integrate its scattering, the view rays as well as the
sunlight reaching each point in the air. Along a bent ray in an atmosphere that is the same in every direction, n r
sin(z) stays constant, with r the distance from the planet's center and z the angle from the vertical. That alone
decides where the ground starts, which also lifts the visible horizon: from 10 km up, by 0.2°. The same model covers an
observer on the ground, one high up looking down, and one in orbit looking through the planet's rim, where a grazing ray
crosses the air twice and bends by more than a degree.

Near the ground the traced refraction lands within a few percent of Bennett's above 5°. At the horizon it is some 10%
larger: the model's air thins out with one scale height, the real air more slowly near the ground, where the
temperature falls with height. The tables store the result: the sky-view table, indexed by where the ray is seen, how
far it bent; the transmittance table, indexed by where the light comes from, how much of it arrives. So the sky is
looked up, and the sun disc flattens near the horizon and stays in view after it has geometrically set, without either
being a special case.

## Tables instead of raymarching

Three tables carry the sky. Transmittance says how much light gets through the air along any ray, multiple scattering
how much light arrives after more than one bounce, and the sky view holds the whole sky as seen from where the observer
stands, rebuilt whenever the Sun or the observer moves. The sky pass then only looks up one texel per pixel.

<Fig id="lookup" /> follows one of those pixels back. Its view ray is marched from the eye in 30 steps, short ones first
where it goes up, since the air is densest right at the eye, and the haze in it within a kilometer or two. At each, sunlight
comes in from the side: how much of it made it through the air is a transmittance lookup, by the step's altitude and
the Sun's angle there, and the light that bounced more than once is a multiple scattering lookup, by the same two. How
much of all that still reaches the eye is summed up along the way, step by step. Sixty lookups end up in a single texel
of the sky view, and the sky pass reads just that one. Point at a texel below or in <Fig id="tables" />, and all of
them follow the same ray.

## Lighting a scene

The same sky can light a scene. The sky pass writes it into the six faces of a cube map, in cd/m² and without the
sun's disc, and a second pass projects that onto nine spherical harmonics coefficients, which give the irradiance for
any surface's orientation <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />. The disc stays out on purpose: at some two billion cd/m² it is
far brighter than a half float holds, and at half a degree smaller than a texel of all but the largest cube maps, so it
would be one blocky texel, smeared by the mip levels, and the coefficients would ring with it. Wherever the cube map is
shown, the disc is drawn over it instead, sharp at any resolution. The sun lights the scene apart, as a light of its own,
with what the air leaves of it.

Why a cube map, rather than the atmosphere itself for every pixel and every surface? Because the sky hardly changes from
one frame to the next. The Sun moves by a quarter of a degree a minute, its own diameter in about two, and at 60 frames
per second that is thousands of frames each computing the same sky again. So the cube map is built once whenever the Sun
or the observer has moved, and every frame after that only samples it: one filtered fetch per direction, for the
background as for a reflection, and nine coefficients for all of the diffuse light. Lighting needs the whole sky anyway,
not only the part the camera sees. At 128 texels a face, a build computes 98,304 texels, a twentieth of the pixels of a
full HD frame; how long it takes shows above the figure.

A cube map's faces are six perspective views, and a perspective crowds the most directions into its middle: a texel at a
face's corner covers only a fifth of the solid angle of one at its center. So the sky is resolved five times as finely
towards the corners as in the middle of a face, and where two faces meet, the texel grid turns abruptly, which can show
in gradients as smooth as the sky's. Cubified, as osgHimmel's `CubeMappedHimmel` did it <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />,
the texels are placed by Nowell's mapping of a cube onto a sphere <Cite id="nowell2005" /> instead. The largest then
covers 1.31 times the solid angle of the smallest, a little more even than the equi-angular cube map YouTube streams
video in, at 1.41 <Cite id="brown2017" />. It costs a few instructions per lookup, and whoever samples the cube map has
to know: it looks up the point on the cube that maps to its direction, not the direction itself. Filtering across the
faces' edges and the mip levels work as before. The two figures below switch it together.

The cube map comes with its mip levels, each texel the mean of four in the level above, down to a single texel a face.
They cost a third more memory and a few cheap passes, and they are optional: the cube pass fills as many levels as the
texture was created with, so a `mipLevelCount` of 1 leaves them out. Two
readers need them here. The projection onto the coefficients takes 4,096 directions; from a face of 1,024 texels it would
see only one texel in some 1,500 and miss whatever lies between, so it reads the level whose texels are about as many as
its directions. And a background showing the cube map through a wide lens spans several texels with each pixel, so it
reads the level where a texel is about a pixel wide, instead of picking one texel of many and flickering as the view
turns.

## From light to pixels

The sky comes out in physical units: luminance, in candela per square meter. The model's sun has the spectrum
Bruneton's tables use and the illuminance it has above the atmosphere, 128 000 lux: the solar constant of
1361 W/m² <Cite id="kopp2011" /> times about 94 lumens per watt for sunlight. That puts a clear midday sky at some
thousands of cd/m², the sun's disc near 1.6 × 10⁹, and the sky at the end of twilight around a thousandth.

The sky-view table holds them in 16-bit floats, which keep full precision over some 30 stops, from 6 × 10⁻⁵ to 65 504.
The sky spans more than that in a day: its brightest value falls by 37 stops from noon to the sun 30° down, and held in
cd/m², deep twilight would coarsen into steps that an exposure for the night magnifies into colored blocks. So the table
holds the luminance times a power of two that follows the sun, which keeps its brightest value near 2¹⁰ at any hour,
and whatever reads it divides by the same.

Exposure is set in EV100, as on a camera and in engines like Frostbite and Unreal <Cite id="lagarde2014" />: the
luminance that just saturates is 1.2 × 2^EV100 cd/m². A sunny day is about EV 15, an overcast one 10, twilight 0,
moonlight −6. Since the sky is in cd/m², it takes the same exposure as any other scene built on this convention, and
looks the same under it.

Automatic exposure is an option, for a renderer without an exposure of its own, or to follow day into night. A light
meter on the GPU reads the sky-view table in directions spread evenly over the sky above the horizon, and takes the
geometric mean of their luminance, as a camera's averaging meter does: EV100 = log2(L · 100 / 12.5). It meters the
directions below the horizon apart and takes the brighter: on the ground that is the sky, high up the lit air below,
while the sky above turns black, and from space the lit planet, raymarched like the view. No direction counts more than
ten stops below the brightest, a camera's range, so the black sky and the night side do not drown out the day side. The sky pass reads that
straight from the meter's buffer. Metering the table rather than the image means the reading does not change as the
camera turns, and the sun's disc, which is not in the table, does not darken everything. The meter reads again whenever
the table is rebuilt; the exposure follows at once, without the eye's slow adaptation. The day meters around EV 14, a
sunset around 12, and twilight follows down to 4, with the sun 6° below the horizon, where it stops: exposed like day,
the night would look like day, only noisier. On top, a ramp over the day shapes it: compensations at sun altitudes above the observer's horizon, which sinks
as the observer rises, joined by a smooth curve that never overshoots them <Cite id="fritsch1980" />. By default they
keep the night and the minutes around sunrise and sunset darker than metered, lift the blue hour a little so its blue
shows, make the sunset a little brighter than the sunrise, and leave noon as metered. Mornings and evenings may differ,
though the model's air is the same for both.

The floor of EV 4 is for now. A camera exposes a landscape by full moon at about EV −3 and the Milky Way at about −6
to −7. Once the moon lights the air and the stars are drawn, the floor drops and the night follows. Exposed that far
today, the sky would show only the sun's last twilight, amplified beyond what the table can hold.

For a display, the pass then tone maps: exposed, compressed into the display's range, and encoded for sRGB, dithered
against banding. Khronos PBR Neutral <Cite id="khronos2024" /> keeps colors exactly as they are up to three quarters of
white and compresses only the brightest, so the sky's blue stays the blue the model computes. AgX <Cite id="sobotka2023" />,
in Wrensch's fit <Cite id="wrensch2023" />, fades bright colors towards white as film does: the glow around the sun and
a sunset's brightest band turn white instead of clipping to a flat hue, but the daytime sky comes out flatter and
greyer. Narkowicz's fit of the ACES filmic curve <Cite id="narkowicz2016" />, which dunstkreis used first, is the third:
saturated, and it pushes bright hues towards yellow. The exposure follows a camera's convention, which puts a metered
average at about a tenth of white, where ACES expects it; Neutral and AgX expect the scene's middle gray at 0.18, and
are lifted by the difference. The tone curve choice in <Fig id="sky" /> switches every figure between them. A renderer with its own HDR
pipeline turns that off and gets the exposed luminance itself, linear and unclamped, for a float target.

## Code examples

dunstkreis records into a command encoder you own, writing into a texture you own. It creates no device, no canvas and
no render pass of its own, so it slots in before whatever else your frame draws. Every further example builds on this
one.

**Feed it a sky.** Precompute the tables once per atmosphere, create a sky pass for your target, and hand it the Sun and
a camera. The Sun's direction is any unit vector in ENU: sternzeit computes it here, but dunstkreis does not depend on
it, and a fixed vector or your engine's own light does just as well.

```ts
import { createSkyPass, precomputeAtmosphere } from "@himmelszelt/dunstkreis";
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, sun } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Once per atmosphere: the transmittance and multiple scattering tables.
const luts = await precomputeAtmosphere(device);
// Into a canvas configured with rgba8unorm and STORAGE_BINDING usage.
const sky = await createSkyPass(device, { luts, format: "rgba8unorm", autoExposure: true });

// Whenever the moment, the place or the camera changes.
const time = fromDate(new Date());
const observer = { latitude: 52.39, longitude: 13.13, heightM: 10 };
sky.update({
    // Unit vectors in ENU: x east, y north, z up. True, not refracted.
    sunDirection: sun.direction(time, observer),
    sunAngularDiameter: sun.apparentAngularDiameter(julianEphemerisDay(time)),
    observerHeightM: observer.heightM,
    inverseViewProjection, // your camera's without translation, column-major; reversed-z works too
});
const encoder = device.createCommandEncoder();
sky.encode(encoder, context.getCurrentTexture());
device.queue.submit([encoder.finish()]);
```

`update` only rewrites a few numbers when just the camera moves. A new Sun or a new height also rebuilds the table of
the whole sky, which the pass then reads once per pixel.
