## Why is the sky blue?

<Planned title="A short history of the question">
    From Leonardo's blue haze in front of distant mountains through Newton, Tyndall's blue light from fine particles and
    Rayleigh's inverse fourth power to Mie, Smoluchowski and Einstein, after Hoeppe's "Why the Sky Is Blue". A timeline,
    each step with the one idea it added.
</Planned>

<Planned title="How strongly each color scatters">
    The visible spectrum against how much of it a column of air scatters: violet many times more than red. Why the
    sky is still blue and not violet, from the Sun's spectrum and the eye's sensitivity.
</Planned>

<Planned title="Molecules, aerosols and ozone">
    The three actors, illustrated rather than shaded: where a photon goes after meeting an air molecule (Rayleigh, as
    much forward as back), a droplet or dust grain (Mie, a narrow forward lobe, with its asymmetry to drag) and ozone,
    which only absorbs. Their phase functions as live polar plots.
</Planned>

## Why is the sunset red?

<Planned title="The long way through the air">
    One ray from the observer to the top of the atmosphere, the Sun's elevation to drag: how the path lengthens towards
    the horizon, what the optical depth along it is, and how much of each color survives. The transmittance table
    below is the same thing for every height and angle at once.
</Planned>

<Planned title="Taking the sky apart">
    The same sunset with Rayleigh, Mie and ozone switched on and off one by one. Without ozone, twilight turns gray.
</Planned>

## More than one bounce

<Planned title="Scattering orders">
    Light scattered once, twice, three times, side by side, and the difference each order still makes. Bruneton
    iterates a fixed number of orders, Hillaire sums all of them at once under the assumption that light scattered more
    than once no longer has a preferred direction.
</Planned>

## Bent light

<Planned title="With and without refraction">
    The setting Sun side by side, bent and unbent, zoomed in on the horizon: the disc flattened, still in view while
    geometrically already below the horizon, and the altitude grid to measure it against.
</Planned>

## Luftperspektive

<Planned title="Distance in the air">
    Mountain ridges one behind another, with and without the air between them: contrast fades and colors shift
    towards the sky's with every kilometer, the cue painters since Leonardo use to paint depth. A small game: how far
    away is this ridge?
</Planned>

<Planned title="Beyond the atmosphere">
    The Moon, planets or a spaceship behind the atmosphere get the same treatment: dimmed and tinted by the air in
    front of them, and lit by sunlight that crossed the atmosphere on its way to them.
</Planned>

## From the ground to orbit

<Planned title="Leaving the ground">
    The observer from a millimeter above the ground up into orbit: the horizon dips, the sky darkens overhead, and
    from above the atmosphere the whole planet appears, its thin glowing rim and the terminator between day and night.
</Planned>

## Tables instead of raymarching

<Planned title="The tables, live">
    Every table dunstkreis computes, as it is right now: transmittance, multiple scattering and the sky view, later
    aerial perspective and Bruneton's four-dimensional inscatter table. Over each, the grid of its parameterization;
    pointing at a texel shows the ray it stands for.
</Planned>

## Resolution and sampling

<Planned title="Starving the tables">
    Table sizes and sample counts to turn down, next to a reference computed at high resolution, and a heat map of the
    difference: where bands and seams appear first, and why the mappings spend their texels near the horizon.
</Planned>

## Encoding

<Planned title="Storing light">
    The same table stored as 32-bit and 16-bit float, as RGBE and as 8 bits with a logarithmic or gamma curve: what
    each keeps and loses. Why lookups aim at texel centers, how four dimensions are packed into a 3D texture, and which
    of these dunstkreis uses.
</Planned>

## From light to pixels

<Planned title="Exposure and tone mapping">
    The eye adapts across some fourteen orders of magnitude, from starlight to the midday Sun, and a screen shows about
    three. The sky's physical radiance with its exposure to drag, tone curves side by side, and an HDR display where
    the browser offers one.
</Planned>

<Planned title="Banding and dithering">
    A smooth twilight gradient quantized to 8 bits, zoomed in: the bands, and the same with a single step of noise
    added before quantizing.
</Planned>

## Precise or approximate?

<Planned title="Bruneton and Hillaire">
    Both variants side by side at the same moment and place, and their difference.
</Planned>
