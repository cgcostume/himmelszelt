Clouds are the part of the sky that changes by the minute, and the part a renderer gives up on first: a sky is one
lookup per pixel, a cloud is a volume, and light wanders through it. gewoelk renders them anyway, on the GPU, in the
way Guerrilla's Nubis³ renders the clouds a player flies through in Horizon Forbidden West
<Cite id="schneider2023" />: the low clouds live in a <Term of="Voxel">voxel</Term> volume with a
<Term of="Signed distance field">distance field</Term> that tells each ray how far it may step, every voxel knows how
much cloud lies between it and the Sun before a single pixel is drawn, and detail noise turns coarse voxels into
billows and wisps. Where Nubis³ models its clouds in Houdini, gewoelk grows them from a
<Term of="Cloud map">cloud map</Term>, the two-dimensional field osgHimmel drew its clouds from
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />, so they can change shape and drift with the wind. Above them a thin sheet of
<Term of="Cirrus">cirrus</Term> is read once where a ray crosses it. The sky behind, the Sun's light at the ground and
the light of the sky come from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) here, as plain numbers: gewoelk takes a direction, lux and
irradiance, from wherever a renderer has them.

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, WebGPU compute passes, no dependency on any other package at runtime. One pass
builds the volume whenever the clouds change shape, one sums the light whenever the Sun moves, and one marches the
clouds per pixel, at a share of the frame's resolution if the budget asks for it, and composes them over your sky in
cd/m², tone mapped or linear. What that costs on your GPU is on the line under <Fig id="clouds" />.

Drag to look around, scroll to zoom, and set the moment and the place under it, which the clouds, the Sun and the sky
follow. The sliders in the panel shape the clouds and switch the parts of their light. The figure is exposed a stop
brighter than [dunstkreis' light meter](../dunstkreis/#from-light-to-pixels) reads the clear sky, as a photographer
exposes for clouds: metered as they come, bright clouds turn gray.

## Two skies, three models

Look up on a fair day and there are two skies of cloud: the <Term of="Cumulus">heaps</Term> a few kilometers up,
flat below and billowing above, and far above them the veils and streaks of cirrus, ice crystals 6 to 12 km up that
hardly move and hardly shade. The two ask for different renderers. A sheet of cirrus is thin: a ray crosses it once,
and what it takes from the Sun and what it adds is one lookup into its cloud map, the map's value read as an
<Term of="Optical depth">optical depth</Term>, thicker at a slant, and the light forward scattered by a narrow
<Term of="Phase function">phase function</Term> <Cite id="henyey1941" />, which is why the Sun behind cirrus has a
glow. The heaps are volumes, and the rest of this chapter is about them.

gewoelk renders clouds by three models, told apart here by the dimensions each holds. A sheet is 2D: one map on a
shell at one height, read once. A height field is 2.5D: a map again, but every texel of it is a column of cloud with
a bottom and a top, and a ray steps through the columns. It holds no overhang, only what noise heaps onto its
surface. A voxel volume is 3D: a grid of densities that holds any shape, and pays for it in memory. The figures carry
these marks, 2D, 2.5D and 3D, wherever they offer a cloud.

## From a map to a volume

osgHimmel modeled its clouds as a height field: octaves of Perlin's gradient noise <Cite id="perlin2002" /> summed
into one tileable map, shaped by a coverage, which decides how much of it is sky, and a sharpness, which hardens the
edges, and each value read as a height, so a value of 1 is a column reaching the top of the layer and a small value a
wisp. One trick made them look like cumulus: the bottoms dip below the layer's base level by only a fraction of the
value, the offset, while the tops rise by the rest, so the bottoms stay nearly flat where the tops billow
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />. Real cumulus are flat below for a reason: that is the height where rising air has
cooled enough for its water to condense, the same for the whole sky that day.

gewoelk keeps the map and the trick, and voxelizes the result: a grid of 256 by 256 by 32 voxels over a slab 16 km
across and 2.4 km tall, which repeats east and north so it reaches the horizon, each voxel inside or outside the
height field. From that grid a <Term of="Jump flooding">jump flood</Term> finds, for every voxel, the nearest voxel of
the clouds' surface, in six passes, and the distance to it becomes Nubis³'s modeling data: outside the clouds the
signed distance, stored in 8 bits and rounded down so no step ever overshoots; inside, the
<Term of="Dimensional profile">dimensional profile</Term>, a gradient from 0 at the surface to 1 some 150 m in, which
is both the shape the detail noise erodes and, later, the chance that light scatters there. All of it is rebuilt on the
GPU whenever the map evolves, which is what Nubis³ left for the future: its distance fields were baked
<Cite id="schneider2023" />.

## Billows and wisps

Nubis³'s voxels are 8 m across and the clouds still look soft up close, because the shape is not the voxels': at every
sample the profile is eroded by a composite of detail noise, and the noise carries the detail
<Cite id="schneider2023" />. Two kinds of it, in one tiling 3D texture of 128 texels a side: billows, where vapor
pushes out through weak spots and the surface rounds into lumps, and wisps, where the surrounding air eats into the
cloud and leaves webs and curls. Both are cut with <Term of="Alligator noise">alligator noise</Term>, a cellular noise
from Houdini <Cite id="sidefx2023" />: one random point in each cell, a smooth bump around each, and the largest bump
less the second largest, which draws ridges between the cells. As it is, it makes billows; inverted and swirled by a
curl field, wisps. Each at a low and a high frequency, blended by the profile so the core stays coarse and the surface
fine, and the two kinds blended by a type field from the map, so one cloud is billowy and its neighbor frayed. A power
at the end sharpens the thin parts. gewoelk's voxels are some 60 m across, eight times Nubis³'s, and the same erosion
has to carry correspondingly more.

## Light in a cloud

A cloud is bright on the side the Sun lights and gray underneath, and that is almost the whole story of its light: of
the sunlight entering it, what reaches a point inside falls off with the amount of cloud in the way, by the
<Term of="Beer-Lambert law">Beer-Lambert law</Term> <Cite id="beer1852" />, and what scatters from there towards the
eye depends on the angle, by the phase function. A raymarcher that is honest about it marches a second ray from every
sample towards the Sun, and that second march is what makes volumetric clouds expensive <Cite id="schneider2015" />.
Nubis³ cuts it loose: for every voxel that holds cloud, the density to the Sun is summed once, in a pass of its own,
and the march reads the sum. gewoelk sums it to the sky above and the ground below as well, so the ambient light fades
with depth too. The sum is coarse, so the first two samples towards the Sun are still taken in full detail, a quarter
and three quarters of a voxel on, which keeps the edges crisp.

Light scattered once is not enough: a sunlit cloud top is nearly white, and single scattering with a forward lobe
gives it a dull gray, since little of that lobe points at the eye. The light that has scattered two, three, many times
is what fills it in, and gewoelk takes it as octaves, the trick from the film Oz <Cite id="wrenninge2013" /> that
Frostbite brought to games <Cite id="hillaire2016" />: each order a share of the one before, through less cloud, with
a lobe half as narrow, eight of them. The share may never be larger than the factor the cloud thins by, or the octaves
would make light out of nothing. The sky's light fades with depth by its own sum alone, not by the profile as well,
as in Nubis³: that counts the depth twice and draws dark bands between the sky-lit tops and the ground-lit bases.
Switch the three parts in <Fig id="clouds" />, the Sun, the multiple scattering and the ambient light, to see what
each one adds: the Sun alone gives a cloud with black shadows, the multiple scattering its glow, the ambient light its
gray underside. The Sun's light reaches the clouds at their own altitude, not the observer's: after sunset the
ground lies in shadow while a cloud base 2 km up is still lit, from below, by a reddened Sun, and the summed density
towards it runs downward through the volume then. Step the moment past sunset and watch the undersides turn.

Where a cloud thins out in front of the Sun or the Moon, it glows: a disc of light a few degrees wide, bluish white
with a brownish red rim, the <Term of="Corona">corona</Term>. Its droplets bend light around themselves as small discs
do, into a lobe as wide as the wavelength is to the droplet, so red spreads farther than blue
<Cite id="cowley2005" />. Half of what a droplet takes out of a beam goes this way <Cite id="vandehulst1957" />, so
nearly straight on that a cloud renderer, gewoelk too, counts it as light passed through. The composition spreads that
half around its source again wherever the clouds let some light through: most where three fifths of it pass, none in
clear air or behind thick cloud. `corona` sets the droplets' radius, 10 µm by default for a glow 2° in radius, some
seven Moons across, and how far their sizes vary, since only droplets all of one size draw rings around it. The Moon's
corona takes its direction and its light, `moonDirection` and `moonIlluminance`; the clouds themselves are lit by the
Sun alone so far.

## Marching by the distance field

Without knowing where the clouds are, a march samples empty sky at the same pace as cloud. Nubis³ steps by the
distance field instead, the <Term of="Sphere tracing">sphere tracing</Term> of John Hart <Cite id="hart1996" />:
outside a cloud the step is the distance to the nearest surface, which no cloud lies within, and the ray crosses the
gaps in a handful of steps. Inside, where the distance is negative, the step grows with the square root of the distance
from the camera, 15 m at the first cloud and some 30 m ten kilometers out, finer than a pixel covers there.
The first step is jittered by a pattern that changes every frame, so the bands a fixed step would draw turn into noise.
Nubis³ shipped at 2.2 to 4 ms at 960 by 540 pixels on a PlayStation 5, from the ground and in flight, and halved it at
60 Hz by marching the near clouds at a quarter of the pixels and the far ones at all of them
<Cite id="schneider2023" />. gewoelk marches the whole sky at one scale of your choosing and composes it bilinearly;
the two-pass split is on its list. Set the sampling in the figure and watch the time on the line under it.

What a frame costs hangs on a few numbers, and `cloudQuality` gathers them into three dials of four levels each, low
to ultra, for both models. Reach is how far the clouds are seen before they thin out to a clear horizon. Sampling is
the share of the frame the clouds are marched at, a ray's steps, and the sizes of the volumes, maps and noise. Motion
is how the shape changes: not at all, where the clouds still drift and billow for free, by keyframes built a few steps
a frame, or every frame. One level sets all three, or each takes its own, and what comes back are the options the
passes are made with.

The voxel volume has a figure of its own, where its numbers are set on a patch of it alone
(<Fig id="design-voxel" />): a drawing of the model, the patch seen from a camera that turns about it, the sliders,
and under them the cloud map and the volumes built from it, each a slice at a time. Raise the towers, and columns of
the grid grow through the slab while the map stays as it was. The library names its kinds of voxel cloud by their
species, `CLOUDSCAPE_TYPES`: three sizes of cumulus, humilis, mediocris and congestus, a deck of
<Term of="Stratocumulus">stratocumulus</Term>, and <Term of="Stratus">stratus</Term>. Each comes with the grid that
holds it without waste, since a flat layer needs a fraction of the levels a tower does.

## A kind of cloud for each place

Weather observers sort clouds by where they stand: low heaps and decks, towers that grow through every level, a middle
layer, and the ice clouds on top. gewoelk's layered model has these four places. It marches the first three as one
density, with nothing built but a map for each, 2.5D, and reads the fourth as a sheet, 2D. For each place the library
names a handful of kinds, `CLOUD_TYPES`, each a few numbers over the place's defaults. The five figures of this section are
where a kind is made. Each has three parts: a drawing of the model that kind of cloud is rendered by, a patch of the
cloud alone, seen from above, and its numbers as sliders, and under them the textures it is rendered from, each with
its format, its size and what it takes on the GPU. The patch stands under the
sky of the chosen date and place, at a time of day of its own: play runs its clock, as fast as a clock, a minute a
second or an hour a second, and the clouds drift and change with it. The library's kinds are there to start from, and
up to eight of your own are kept in your browser under the names you give them. The copy button puts the numbers as
they stand on the clipboard, written as an entry for `CLOUD_TYPES`.

The low layer holds what a fair day brings, heaps with flat bases 1.2 km up, and with more of the sky covered the
same map closes into a deck of <Term of="Stratocumulus">stratocumulus</Term> (<Fig id="design-low" />). Raise the
coverage step by step, and the gaps between the heaps close.

## Rainbows

A rainbow is not a thing in the rain but an angle. Stand with the Sun at your back, and every drop that happens to be
42° from the shadow of your head sends you color, whichever drop that is at the moment. The shadow marks the
<Term of="Antisolar point">antisolar point</Term>, straight opposite the Sun, and the bow is a circle around it. The
42° come from following rays of sunlight through a drop, as Descartes did in 1637 <Cite id="descartes1637" />: each is
refracted on its way in, reflected off the back and refracted again on its way out, and how far that turns it depends
on where it met the drop, its <Term of="Impact parameter">impact parameter</Term>. A ray through the middle comes
straight back, turned by 180°. Rays farther out are turned less, but never less than 138°, and near that least
deviation a whole bundle of neighbors leaves side by side (<Fig id="raindrop" />). There the light piles up, 180° less
138° from the antisolar point: a <Term of="Caustic">caustic</Term>, like the bright lines on the bottom of a pool.
Drag the ray from the middle of the drop to its rim, and the angle at the eye grows to 42°, then shrinks again.

<Drop />

Water bends blue light more than red: its <Term of="Refractive index">refractive index</Term> rises from 1.330 at
700 nm to 1.343 at 400 nm, which `waterIndex` has from Daimon and Masumura's measurements <Cite id="daimon2007" />.
So every wavelength has a least deviation of its own, and `bowRadius` puts red at 42.5° and violet at 40.6°, a band
three and a half Suns wide. Light reflected twice inside the drop makes the secondary bow at 51°, a seventh as bright
and with its colors the other way round. Between the two bows no light of either kind arrives:
<Term of="Alexander's band">Alexander's band</Term>, named after Alexander of Aphrodisias, who taught in Athens around
the year 200 <Cite id="jackson1999" />. This is where a painted bow goes wrong. A rainbow is the bright rim of a disc
of light, not a colored line on the sky: the rain just inside the primary bow sends back over twenty times the light
of the band outside it.

Rays are not the whole story. By them the bow's rim would be infinitely bright, and they know nothing of the pale
fringes sometimes seen just inside it, the <Term of="Supernumerary bows">supernumerary bows</Term>. Those are waves at
work: two rays that entered the drop on either side of the least deviated one leave it the same way after paths of
different lengths, and strengthen or cancel each other, as Thomas Young saw in 1803. George Airy worked the whole bow
out as a wave, published in 1838 <Cite id="airy1838" /> <Cite id="jackson1999" />. `rainbowTable` follows Airy for both
bows and plain rays for what a drop reflects off its outside and lets through, summed over the visible wavelengths,
over drops of many sizes and over the Sun's disc, half a degree that blurs every fringe. What comes out is rain's phase
function as a row of colors over the <Term of="Scattering angle">scattering angle</Term>
(<Fig id="rain-phase" />). Most of the light, 94%, goes on forward past the drop, and next to that the bow is a small
thing: at its crest a drop sends 1.6 times what it would if it scattered evenly to all sides.

A fringe is as wide as its drop is small, so the drops decide what a bow looks like. A shower holds drops of many
sizes at once <Cite id="marshall1948" />, their fringes fall on top of each other, and what is left is the smooth bow
everyone knows. Set the drops to 0.15 mm and their spread to one size, and supernumerary bows appear inside the
primary. Go down to 0.02 mm, droplets as in fog, and every color spreads so wide that they merge into a pale, nearly
white <Term of="Fogbow">fogbow</Term>, 2° farther in.

<Phase />

Where a bow stands is geometry alone. The antisolar point lies as far under the horizon as the Sun stands over it, so
the primary bow's top is 42° less the Sun's elevation up, which is `bowTop`: half a circle at sunset, a low arc in the
afternoon, and nothing once the Sun is higher than 42° (<Fig id="bow-place" />). Drag the Sun up, and both bows sink
into the ground, the primary gone with the Sun 42° up and the secondary at 51°. That is why rainbows belong to mornings
and evenings: on June 21 in Potsdam the Sun is too high for a primary bow from 9:46 to 16:33 local time. The rest of
the circle lies under the horizon, and only an observer with rain beneath, on a mountain or in a plane, has drops there
to see it in. Drag the Sun down to the horizon instead, and the rendered bow turns red: the air has taken the blue out
of the light that makes it.

<BowPlace />

For a renderer all of this is one lookup per view ray. `antisolarAngle` is the ray's angle from the antisolar point,
the table at 180° less it gives red, green and blue per steradian, and times the Sun's illuminance and the rain's
optical depth along the ray, as long as that is thin, it is light in cd/m² to add over the sky behind.
`rainDropRadius` and `rainExtinction` turn a rain rate into the drops' size and that depth, by Marshall and Palmer's
sizes. The table takes some 50 ms to compute and changes only with the rain; a shader reads it as one row of texels
through `gwRainbowPhase` in `wgsl.rainbow`. The view ray, the Sun's direction and illuminance and the kilometers of
sunlit rain along the ray are yours:

```ts
import {
    antisolarAngle,
    rainbowPhase,
    rainbowTable,
    rainDropRadius,
    rainExtinction,
} from "@himmelszelt/gewoelk";

const rate = 5; // mm/h, a shower: drops of 0.52 mm
const table = rainbowTable({ dropRadiusMm: rainDropRadius(rate) });

const angle = 180 - antisolarAngle(viewDirection, sunDirection);
const depth = rainExtinction(rate) * sunlitKm; // 0.25 through 500 m
const [red, green, blue] = rainbowPhase(table, angle).map(
    (phase) => phase * sunIlluminance * depth,
);
// On the bow's crest under 100,000 lux: 3,800 cd/m² in green.
```

## The people behind the numbers

Andrew Schneider modeled clouds for the animated film Rio with fluid simulation and spent the next decade getting them
into a game. Nubis began in 2015 with Horizon Zero Dawn, the first real-time cloudscape that raymarched noise
<Cite id="schneider2015" />, and Nubis³ in 2023 turned the voxel clouds of film into something a player flies through
<Cite id="schneider2023" />. John Hart named sphere tracing in 1996, for the ray tracing of implicit surfaces
<Cite id="hart1996" />; distance fields have carried it into every demo scene since. Louis Henyey and Jesse
Greenstein were astronomers, and their phase function of 1941 described the dust of the Milky Way
<Cite id="henyey1941" /> before it described every cloud in every game. August Beer measured how red light faded in
colored liquids in 1852 <Cite id="beer1852" />, and Ken Perlin improved his own noise in 2002 <Cite id="perlin2002" />,
seventeen years after he introduced it; the original had won him an Academy Award in 1997.

The rainbow's numbers are older. René Descartes traced rays through a drop in 1637 and found the angle they crowd at
<Cite id="descartes1637" />, and two centuries on George Airy treated the same drop as a wave, in a paper on the light
near a caustic whose function still carries his name <Cite id="airy1838" />. The water in `waterIndex` was measured by
Masahiko Daimon and Akira Masumura in 2007, from the near infrared into the ultraviolet <Cite id="daimon2007" />, and
the sizes of raindrops are J. S. Marshall and W. McK. Palmer's of 1948, from a paper two pages long
<Cite id="marshall1948" />. John David Jackson, the author of Classical Electrodynamics, told the bow's whole story
from Alexander to Airy in ten <Cite id="jackson1999" />.
