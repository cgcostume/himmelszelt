Clouds are the part of the sky that changes by the minute, and the part a renderer gives up on first: a sky is one
lookup per pixel, a cloud is a volume, and light wanders through it. gewoelk renders them anyway, on the GPU, in the
way Guerrilla's Nubis³ renders the clouds a player flies through in Horizon Forbidden West
<Cite id="schneider2023" />: the low clouds live in a <Term of="Voxel">voxel</Term> volume with a
<Term of="Signed distance field">distance field</Term> that tells each ray how far it may step, every voxel knows how
much cloud lies between it and the Sun before a single pixel is drawn, and detail noise turns coarse voxels into
billows and wisps. Where Nubis³ models its clouds in Houdini, gewoelk grows them from a
<Term of="Cloud map">cloud map</Term>, the two-dimensional field osgHimmel drew its clouds from
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />, so they can change shape and drift with the wind. Above them a thin sheet of
<Term of="Cirrus">cirrus</Term> is read once where a ray crosses it. The sky behind, the Sun's light at the ground and
the light of the sky come from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) here, as plain numbers: gewoelk takes a direction, lux and
irradiance, from wherever a renderer has them.

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, WebGPU compute passes, no dependency on any other package at runtime. One pass
builds the volume whenever the clouds change shape, one sums the light whenever the Sun moves, and one marches the
clouds per pixel, at a share of the frame's resolution if the budget asks for it, and composes them over your sky in
cd/m², tone mapped or linear. What that costs on your GPU is on the line under <Fig id="clouds" />.

Drag to look around, scroll to zoom, and set the moment and the place under it, which the clouds, the Sun and the sky
follow. The sliders in the panel shape the clouds and switch the parts of their light. The figure is exposed a stop
brighter than [dunstkreis' light meter](../dunstkreis/#from-light-to-pixels) reads the clear sky, as a photographer
exposes for clouds: metered as they come, bright clouds turn gray.

## Two kinds of cloud

Look up on a fair day and there are two skies of cloud: the <Term of="Cumulus">heaps</Term> a few kilometers up,
flat below and billowing above, and far above them the veils and streaks of cirrus, ice crystals 6 to 12 km up that
hardly move and hardly shade. The two ask for different renderers. A sheet of cirrus is thin: a ray crosses it once,
and what it takes from the Sun and what it adds is one lookup into its cloud map, the map's value read as an
<Term of="Optical depth">optical depth</Term>, thicker at a slant, and the light forward scattered by a narrow
<Term of="Phase function">phase function</Term> <Cite id="henyey1941" />, which is why the Sun behind cirrus has a
glow. The heaps are volumes, and the rest of this chapter is about them.

## From a map to a volume

osgHimmel modeled its clouds as a height field: octaves of Perlin's gradient noise <Cite id="perlin2002" /> summed
into one tileable map, shaped by a coverage, which decides how much of it is sky, and a sharpness, which hardens the
edges, and each value read as a height, so a value of 1 is a column reaching the top of the layer and a small value a
wisp. One trick made them look like cumulus: the bottoms dip below the layer's base level by only a fraction of the
value, the offset, while the tops rise by the rest, so the bottoms stay nearly flat where the tops billow
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />. Real cumulus are flat below for a reason: that is the height where rising air has
cooled enough for its water to condense, the same for the whole sky that day.

gewoelk keeps the map and the trick, and voxelizes the result: a grid of 256 by 256 by 32 voxels over a slab 16 km
across and 2.4 km tall, which repeats east and north so it reaches the horizon, each voxel inside or outside the
height field. From that grid a <Term of="Jump flooding">jump flood</Term> finds, for every voxel, the nearest voxel of
the clouds' surface, in eight passes, and the distance to it becomes Nubis³'s modeling data: outside the clouds the
signed distance, stored in 8 bits and rounded down so no step ever overshoots; inside, the
<Term of="Dimensional profile">dimensional profile</Term>, a gradient from 0 at the surface to 1 some 150 m in, which
is both the shape the detail noise erodes and, later, the chance that light scatters there. All of it is rebuilt on the
GPU whenever the map evolves, which is what Nubis³ left for the future: its distance fields were baked
<Cite id="schneider2023" />.

## Billows and wisps

Nubis³'s voxels are 8 m across and the clouds still look soft up close, because the shape is not the voxels': at every
sample the profile is eroded by a composite of detail noise, and the noise carries the detail
<Cite id="schneider2023" />. Two kinds of it, in one tiling 3D texture of 128 texels a side: billows, where vapor
pushes out through weak spots and the surface rounds into lumps, and wisps, where the surrounding air eats into the
cloud and leaves webs and curls. Both are cut with <Term of="Alligator noise">alligator noise</Term>, a cellular noise
from Houdini <Cite id="sidefx2023" />: one random point in each cell, a smooth bump around each, and the largest bump
less the second largest, which draws ridges between the cells. As it is, it makes billows; inverted and swirled by a
curl field, wisps. Each at a low and a high frequency, blended by the profile so the core stays coarse and the surface
fine, and the two kinds blended by a type field from the map, so one cloud is billowy and its neighbor frayed. A power
at the end sharpens the thin parts. gewoelk's voxels are some 60 m across, eight times Nubis³'s, and the same erosion
has to carry correspondingly more.

## Light in a cloud

A cloud is bright on the side the Sun lights and gray underneath, and that is almost the whole story of its light: of
the sunlight entering it, what reaches a point inside falls off with the amount of cloud in the way, by the
<Term of="Beer-Lambert law">Beer-Lambert law</Term> <Cite id="beer1852" />, and what scatters from there towards the
eye depends on the angle, by the phase function. A raymarcher that is honest about it marches a second ray from every
sample towards the Sun, and that second march is what makes volumetric clouds expensive <Cite id="schneider2015" />.
Nubis³ cuts it loose: for every voxel that holds cloud, the density to the Sun is summed once, in a pass of its own,
and the march reads the sum. gewoelk sums it to the sky above and the ground below as well, so the ambient light fades
with depth too. The sum is coarse, so the first two samples towards the Sun are still taken in full detail, half a
voxel and one and a half voxels on, which keeps the edges crisp.

Light scattered once is not enough: a sunlit cloud top is nearly white, and single scattering with a forward lobe
gives it a dull gray, since little of that lobe points at the eye. The light that has scattered two, three, many times
is what fills it in, and gewoelk takes it as octaves, the trick from the film Oz <Cite id="wrenninge2013" /> that
Frostbite brought to games <Cite id="hillaire2016" />: each order a share of the one before, through less cloud, with
a lobe half as narrow, eight of them. The share may never be larger than the factor the cloud thins by, or the octaves
would make light out of nothing. The sky's light reaches a point the more the closer it lies to the surface, as in
Nubis³: the square root of one minus the profile, the profile saying how deep the point lies.
Switch the three parts in <Fig id="clouds" />, the Sun, the multiple scattering and the ambient light, to see what
each one adds: the Sun alone gives a cloud with black shadows, the multiple scattering its glow, the ambient light its
gray underside. The Sun's light reaches the clouds at their own altitude, not the observer's: after sunset the
ground lies in shadow while a cloud base 2 km up is still lit, from below, by a reddened Sun, and the summed density
towards it runs downward through the volume then. Step the moment past sunset and watch the undersides turn.

## Marching by the distance field

Without knowing where the clouds are, a march samples empty sky at the same pace as cloud. Nubis³ steps by the
distance field instead, the <Term of="Sphere tracing">sphere tracing</Term> of John Hart <Cite id="hart1996" />:
outside a cloud the step is the distance to the nearest surface, which no cloud lies within, and the ray crosses the
gaps in a handful of steps. Inside, where the distance is negative, the step grows with the square root of the distance
from the camera, 15 m at the first cloud and some 30 m ten kilometers out, finer than a pixel covers there.
The first step is jittered by a pattern that changes every frame, so the bands a fixed step would draw turn into noise.
Nubis³ shipped at 2.2 to 4 ms at 960 by 540 pixels on a PlayStation 5, from the ground and in flight, and halved it at
60 Hz by marching the near clouds at a quarter of the pixels and the far ones at all of them
<Cite id="schneider2023" />. gewoelk marches the whole sky at one scale of your choosing and composes it bilinearly;
the two-pass split is on its list. Set the render scale and the steps in the figure and watch the time on the line
under it.

## The people behind the numbers

Andrew Schneider modeled clouds for the animated film Rio with fluid simulation and spent the next decade getting them
into a game. Nubis began in 2015 with Horizon Zero Dawn, the first real-time cloudscape that raymarched noise
<Cite id="schneider2015" />, and Nubis³ in 2023 turned the voxel clouds of film into something a player flies through
<Cite id="schneider2023" />. John Hart named sphere tracing in 1996, for the ray tracing of implicit surfaces
<Cite id="hart1996" />; distance fields have carried it into every demo scene since. Louis Henyey and Jesse
Greenstein were astronomers, and their phase function of 1941 described the dust of the Milky Way
<Cite id="henyey1941" /> before it described every cloud in every game. August Beer measured how red light faded in
colored liquids in 1852 <Cite id="beer1852" />, and Ken Perlin improved his own noise in 2002 <Cite id="perlin2002" />,
twenty years after the original won him an Academy Award.
