Look at a full Moon and it is a coin, not a ball: as bright at its very edge as in its middle, with no shading to round
it off. Every ball a renderer shades the usual way darkens towards its rim. The Moon does not, because its surface is
dust, and dust reflects light by a law of its own. nachtgestirn draws the Moon by that law, Bruce Hapke's
<Cite id="hapke2012" />, over NASA's maps of its color and its relief <Cite id="svs4720" />: lit by the Sun, by
<Term of="Earthshine">earthshine</Term> on its night side and, during an eclipse, by the copper light Earth's air bends
into its shadow. Where the Moon stands and how it is turned comes from [sternzeit](../sternzeit/); the sky behind it
and the air in front of it from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/).

`@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn` is TypeScript and WGSL: one WebGPU compute pass, with no other package needed at runtime.
It takes directions, angles and lux as plain inputs and draws into a texture you own, over a sky of yours or alone,
with its coverage in alpha. Everything is in physical units, so the Moon exposes like the sky around it, by day as by
night, and the pass costs a fraction of a millisecond for the Moon as a sky shows it. What it is for: [the Moon in your
sky](#the-moon-tonight) first of all, [an eclipse](#earths-shadow) as its night shows it, a Moon to turn and zoom
into, and a dusty ball of any kind, since Hapke wrote his law for the airless bodies of the solar system, not for the
Moon alone.

The Moon below is that pass with the sky and the air taken away, out of your sky and into your hands: the Sun where it
stands at the chosen moment, the night side in earthshine, mountains and crater rims casting their shadows along the
<Term of="Terminator">terminator</Term>, the line between day and night. Drag it round onto the far side, which no one
on Earth has ever seen in the sky, and zoom in until single craters fill the view.

In code, it takes one install, the maps and the pass once, and a handful of numbers per frame, which sternzeit
computes:

```sh
npm install @himmelszelt/nachtgestirn @himmelszelt/sternzeit
```

```ts
import {
    createMoonPass, loadMoonMaps, moonEV100, moonOrientation, MOON_MAPS,
} from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, moon } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const maps = await loadMoonMaps(device, MOON_MAPS["1k"]);
const pass = await createMoonPass(device, { format: "rgba8unorm", maps });

// Per frame, from where you stand: where the Moon is, how large, how lit,
// how turned, and an exposure for a sunlit rock.
const time = fromDate(new Date());
const jde = julianEphemerisDay(time);
const obs = { latitude, longitude };
const direction = moon.direction(time, obs);
pass.update({
    direction,
    angularDiameter: moon.topocentricAngularDiameter(time, obs),
    sunDirection: moon.sunDirection(time, obs),
    orientation: moonOrientation({
        direction,
        libration: moon.topocentricLibrations(time, obs),
        positionAngleOfAxis: moon.topocentricPositionAngleOfAxis(time, obs),
        parallacticAngle: moon.parallacticAngle(time, obs),
    }),
    ev100: moonEV100(moon.phaseAngle(jde), moon.illuminatedFraction(jde)),
    inverseViewProjection, // your camera, as dunstkreis' sky pass takes it
    projectionDistance, // its bend towards a fisheye: 0 none, 1 stereographic
});
pass.encode(encoder, target, { background: sky }); // the sky in cd/m²
```

The sections below take these inputs one at a time, from the sky down to the code: the Moon's face and how it is

## One face, almost

The Moon has shown us the same face for as long as anyone has looked up: it turns once for every trip around Earth.
Almost the same face. Over a month it nods and sways a few degrees each way, the
<Term of="Libration">libration</Term>, and lets us peek around its edge. And the face is turned in your sky, like a
clock face held at an angle: a Moon that rises leaning to one side sets leaning to the other.

sternzeit computes the angles behind both, [and its chapter explains them](../sternzeit/#the-moon): the libration, the
<Term of="Position angle of axis">position angle of the Moon's axis</Term> against the sky's north, and the
<Term of="Parallactic angle">parallactic angle</Term> from the sky's north to your own up. nachtgestirn folds them
into one rotation, `moonOrientation`, and from then on knows for every pixel of the disc which point of the Moon it
shows, in <Term of="Selenographic coordinates">selenographic coordinates</Term>, the Moon's own longitude and
latitude. It is the one input of the quick start that is not a single call to sternzeit:

```ts
import { moonOrientation } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";
import { fromDate, moon } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const time = fromDate(new Date());
const obs = { latitude, longitude };
// From your frame into the Moon's own, east, north and up on the Moon.
const orientation = moonOrientation({
    direction: moon.direction(time, obs),
    libration: moon.topocentricLibrations(time, obs),
    positionAngleOfAxis: moon.topocentricPositionAngleOfAxis(time, obs),
    parallacticAngle: moon.parallacticAngle(time, obs),
});
```

A quick check that the face sits right is Mare Crisium, the lone dark oval near the edge. It lies in the Moon's east,
the side where the Sun rises on it, and that is the right of the disc with north up. sternzeit's
[Moon view](../sternzeit/#moon-view) shows it at a switch, nachtgestirn's Moon under its grid: the grid's center is the
point of the Moon that faces Earth, and it wanders across the <Term of="Mare">maria</Term> as the libration nods the
face.

The stricter test is a photograph. Laid over one, for the moment and the place it was taken, nachtgestirn's Moon shows
the same maria and craters where the camera found them (<Fig id="photo" />), and it only does so with the
photographer's own libration. Over a night the face turns by up to a degree more, as Earth's turning carries you from
one side of its center to the other: the diurnal libration, which sternzeit's topocentric functions include. Drawn as
from Earth's center, the craters would sit up to 30 km off at the middle of the disc, and the photographs show that
much. Only the turn of the whole picture is the photographer's choice, so the figure turns each photograph to match.

<Photo />

How large the Moon is comes as a plain angle, `moon.topocentricAngularDiameter`, so the disc follows the orbit without
the pass knowing of one. At <Term of="Perigee">perigee</Term>, nearest Earth, it is 33.5 arcminutes wide, at
<Term of="Apogee">apogee</Term> 29.4: the 14% between a supermoon and a micromoon, and a full Moon near perigee gives a
third more light than one near apogee. The <Term of="Topocentric">topocentric</Term> in the name matters too. From where
you stand, the Moon overhead is an Earth radius nearer than the Moon on the horizon, and almost 2% wider. So the rising
Moon is the smaller one, whatever it looks like: the <Term of="Moon illusion">Moon illusion</Term> is the mind's, and a
renderer that draws the Moon larger on the horizon draws the illusion, not the sky.

## Sunlight on dust

Hold a ball up to the Sun and it goes dark towards its edge. That is Lambert's law, the one a renderer reaches for
first: a surface that scatters light evenly gets less of it the more the light slants, at 80° a sixth of what it gets
face on. The full Moon should show that falloff, and does not. Its surface is a powder, lit and seen through the same
loose layer of grains, and what a slant costs the light on the way in, it gives back on the way out: the
<Term of="Lommel-Seeliger law">Lommel-Seeliger law</Term>. At full Moon the Sun stands behind us, both slants are the
same everywhere on the disc, and they cancel. The center and the rim come out equally bright. The full Moon is flat.

Seen from the side, the difference is how bright a patch of ground looks from each direction (<Fig id="lobes" />): a
Lambert patch looks the same from everywhere, a half circle that only shrinks as the Sun sinks, and a powder looks
brightest from where the Sun stands. Bartosz Ciechanowski's page on the Moon shows this better than any text, a ball of
lunar soil flattening into a disc as the light swings behind you <Cite id="ciechanowski2024" />; this chapter takes from
it only what a renderer needs.

<Lobes />

Bruce Hapke made the powder a law in 1963 <Cite id="hapke1963" />, and added what happens with the Sun right behind
the viewer: every grain then hides its own shadow, and the ground brightens by up to a factor of two, the
<Term of="Opposition surge">opposition surge</Term>. We never see it in full, since a Moon exactly opposite the Sun is
a Moon in Earth's shadow. Jensen and colleagues brought the law into rendering <Cite id="jensen2001" />, and
osgHimmel's Moon followed them <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />. Hapke kept working on it for half a century. Its
modern form <Cite id="hapke2012" /> adds the light scattered from grain to grain and the shadows the ground's slopes
cast on each other, some 24° steep on average <Cite id="hapke1984" />, and Sato and colleagues fitted it to some
66,000 images from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter <Cite id="sato2014" />. nachtgestirn shades by that fit, at no
more cost than the law of 1963.

How bright the result is follows from one more number: seen face on, the Moon reflects 12% of what a white disc
would, its <Term of="Geometric albedo">geometric albedo</Term> <Cite id="nssdc" />. In the 128,000 lux of the Sun
above the air, that makes the full Moon a flat disc of some 4,900 cd/m². It is a sunlit rock, and wants to be exposed
like a landscape at noon.

Which law is right is not a matter of taste, because the Moon's light has been measured. The US Geological Survey's
Robotic Lunar Observatory, ROLO, followed it for over a thousand nights, from a
<Term of="Phase angle">phase angle</Term> of 1.55°, just short of full, to 97°, past the half Moon
<Cite id="kieffer2005" />. Its half Moon gives about a tenth of the light of a full Moon as the sky shows one, not
half, and a fifteenth of what the surge would make of it at its peak: every crater throws a shadow, and the surge is
gone. Held against that curve (<Fig id="phases" />), a Lambert sphere's half Moon comes out
three times too bright, Hapke's law of 1963 a quarter to two fifths of a <Term of="Stop">stop</Term>, and his model of
2012 stays within a fifth of a stop all the way.

<Phases />

Past the measurements, towards the thin crescent, only an older rule is left, Allen's phase law
<Cite id="cox2000" />, and the laws drift apart by stops: how fast the Moon fades there, nobody has measured. The eye
loses the crescent anyway within some 7° of the Sun, in the twilight's glare, which is known as Danjon's limit
<Cite id="schaefer1991" />. Where there is a measurement, the figures' light meter follows it, and exposes the lit
part a stop brighter than mid gray, as a photographer does.

On the disc the same laws show as shading (<Fig id="laws" />). Lambert's rounds the full Moon into a ball with a dark
rim. Oren and Nayar's rough surface <Cite id="oren1994" />, the law a renderer reaches for when Lambert's rim looks
wrong, gets the full Moon nearly flat, but it knows no surge, and its half Moon comes out some two and a half times
too bright. Both of Hapke's keep the full Moon flat to its edge and part at the quarters: the law of 1963 stays
bright up to the terminator, the model of 2012 fades into it. Switch the discs to the day, and the night side
vanishes into a sky as bright as itself, which is [the air's doing](#through-the-air), not the Moon's.

<Laws />

In the shader the law is a single WGSL function, `ngReflectance`, and the pass takes another in its place as
`reflectance`. The package ships the law of 1963 that way, as `wgsl.hapke1963`, and the figures here swap in Lambert's
and Oren and Nayar's the same way.

## Maps and relief

Every Moon in a renderer is a photograph wrapped around a sphere, and which photograph matters. nachtgestirn's maps are
NASA's, from the CGI Moon Kit of its Scientific Visualization Studio <Cite id="svs4720" /> (<Fig id="maps" />). The
color comes from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's wide angle camera: over 100,000 images, each taken with the Sun
high above the ground, joined into one mosaic. The heights come from the orbiter's laser altimeter, which timed a
laser pulse to the ground and back billions of times. Both are laid out with 0° longitude in the middle and east to
the right, so a point's longitude and latitude read straight off them.

<Maps />

The package ships them in four sizes, 512 to 4096 texels around, `MOON_MAPS["512"]` to `MOON_MAPS["4k"]`, from 230 KB
to 11.5 MB. Which one a renderer needs follows from the disc on screen: a map wraps around the whole Moon, so it
stays sharp on a disc up to about a third of its own width. The smallest set does for a disc some 160 pixels across,
the Moon of most skies, the next two for 330 and 650, and the largest is for zooming in. `mapWidthFor` picks the size
for a disc.

Below a texel the ground goes on. A third map holds how rough it is at scales the heights no longer show, from the
altimeter's tracks <Cite id="rosenburg2011" />: the maria smooth, with slopes around 1° over 57 m, the highlands
rough, some 6°, and steeper still around young craters. Where you zoom in past the relief's texels, the pass fills
in slopes as steep as those statistics say the ground is, as noise, down to the pixel.

The color map was made with the Sun high over every part of it, which is what makes it a map and not a picture: it
holds no shadows, so a renderer has to make its own. Most reach for a normal map, as the thesis' Moon did
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />. A normal tilts the shading, but it casts nothing, and the Moon's relief shows by
its shadows. So nachtgestirn ships the heights and
derives the slopes from them on the GPU. Heights are a surface the pass can march through. A normal map is not. They
span 20 km, from 9.2 km below the Moon's mean sphere, in the South Pole-Aitken basin, to 10.8 km above it
<Cite id="smith2010" />.

Most renderers also throw the color away and wrap a gray map around the sphere. Gray is wrong twice
(<Fig id="color" />). The Moon is not gray: it is redder than the sunlight it reflects, a
<Term of="Color index">color index</Term> B-V of 0.92 against the Sun's 0.65 <Cite id="cox2000" />, a warm gray, as
the astronauts photographed it, and a neutral disc over a sky in physical units sits colder than the sky around it.
And the map's small differences in hue are real: the titanium-rich basalt of Mare Tranquillitatis is a little bluer
than the highlands, which a gray map flattens into the same shade of dark. nachtgestirn scales the map to
<Term of="Normal albedo">normal albedo</Term> by the near side's mean and tints it towards the Moon's own color,
keeping its variations; `mapColor` reads it as published, or as gray, for a renderer that wants the paper-white Moon
of the posters.

## Shadows along the terminator

Along the terminator it is sunrise or sunset on the Moon. The Sun stands low, and what the eye sees there is shadow:
crater floors in darkness under rims that catch the light, and peaks beyond the line lit before the plain around
them. The pass casts these shadows by a march (<Fig id="surface" />). From a point of the ground it walks towards the
Sun, in steps that grow as they go, and keeps the highest the terrain rises on the way: the point's horizon. What the
point sees of the Sun is the share of its half-degree disc above that horizon, all of it, some or none. The walk
reaches some 450 km on the largest map, as far as a mountain's shadow runs with the Sun a degree up.

<Surface />

None of this is paid per frame. The march runs only in a band around the terminator, since no crater rim hides a Sun
more than 25° up, and its result goes into a shadow map, kept while the Sun and the view stay where they are. The Sun
crosses the Moon's sky at half a degree an hour, so in a sky one shadow map outlives many frames.

Shading a smooth sphere by the relief's slopes, which is what a normal map does, holds while you look down on the
terrain. Near the <Term of="Limb">limb</Term>, the disc's edge, you look across it: the near rims hide the floors behind
them, and the limb itself is the profile of the mountains standing on it, up to 11 km high, a few pixels on a disc that
fills a screen. So on a large disc the pass marches every ray near the limb through the heights and shades the terrain
where the ray meets it. The globe at the top is drawn that way once you zoom in (<Fig id="globe" />), and so is the
last close-up here (<Fig id="surface" />).

The Sun is a disc, not a point, so the shadows have soft edges, and it matters less than one might think. A
<Term of="Penumbra">penumbra</Term> is a hundredth of its shadow's length: under a kilometer for a shadow 90 km long, a
single pixel on a disc 4096 pixels across. Side by side, far closer in than any sky shows the Moon, a point Sun and the
real one differ by a pixel or two along the shadows' edges (<Fig id="soft-shadows" />). Displacing the relief moves far
more, which the line between the two halves shows as you drag it across. The soft edge comes for free, though: the march
takes the same steps either way and only reads its answer as a share of the Sun instead of a yes or no. So the pass
casts soft shadows whatever the disc's size.

## Earthshine

The night side of a thin crescent glows faintly, lit by sunlight Earth reflects onto it, and it glows most when the
crescent is thinnest, because the Moon then looks at an almost full Earth; sternzeit has [the phenomenon and its
law](../sternzeit/#the-moon). For a renderer, earthshine is a second, very faint light on the Moon, in lux, and
`earthshineIlluminance` gives it: Earth as a Lambert sphere of its geometric albedo, 0.367 <Cite id="nssdc" />, at its
phase as the Moon sees it, which is the Moon's own phase turned around <Cite id="vandehulst1980" />. A full Earth
lights the Moon with some 13 lux, a ten thousandth of sunlight, and about fifty times what a full Moon gives Earth
back: Earth is four times wider and three times brighter.

The night side then shines at half a candela per square meter, thirteen stops below the sunlit crescent. That is why
a photograph exposed for the crescent barely shows it, while the eye, which adapts across the disc, sees it plainly.
Earthlight is not sunlight at second hand, either. It is the light of a blue planet, and the night side measures a
little bluer than the day side, some 15% more blue for its green <Cite id="thejll2014" />. The pass gives earthshine
that tint, so the ashen glow shows a shade colder than the crescent.

```ts
import { earthshineIlluminance } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";
import { moon } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Earth's phase as the Moon sees it is the Moon's own turned around:
// full at new moon, some 13 lux; nothing at full moon.
const distanceKm = moon.distance(jde);
const phaseAngle = moon.phaseAngle(jde);
const earthshine = earthshineIlluminance(180 - phaseAngle, distanceKm);
pass.update({ earthshine, distanceKm });
```

Exposed for the night side, eight stops past the crescent's, it comes up out of the dark with its maria, as in a long
exposure, while a Moon drawn without earthshine stays a hole in the sky (<Fig id="earthshine-crescent" />).

## Earth's shadow

Twice or so a year the full Moon passes through Earth's shadow: first its penumbra, where
Earth covers only part of the Sun, then its <Term of="Umbra">umbra</Term>, where it covers all of it. sternzeit finds
[the eclipses](../sternzeit/#eclipses) and gives the shadow's size. nachtgestirn asks every point of the Moon's
surface what it sees: how much of the Sun's disc Earth's disc hides, the area two circles share. Seen from the Moon,
an eclipse is a sunset behind Earth, and across the disc the shadow's edge sweeps as a soft ramp, never a hard line
(<Fig id="eclipse-row" />). The figure's light meter follows it and opens up as the umbra swallows the disc, until
the copper shows, which is what the eye does too.

<Shadow />

At the Moon's distance the umbra is some 4,600 km in radius, about a Moon's width and a third, and the penumbra
8,200 km. One trap waits for a renderer here: the Moon's place in the shadow has to be taken from Earth's center, not
from where you stand. Your own view shifts the Moon by up to a degree, two of its widths and more than the umbra's
radius. Which is also why an eclipse looks the same from everywhere the Moon is up, only turned with the sky.

The umbra is not black. Earth's air bends sunlight around the planet into the shadow, the light of every sunrise and
sunset on Earth at once, and leaves the eclipsed Moon copper, some ten thousand times fainter than full. How copper
and how bright, nachtgestirn does not set by hand. It adds the light up, after the theory František Link worked out
for the eclipses of his time <Cite id="link1969" />: seen from the Moon, Earth's dark disc wears a thin ring of air,
and every layer of that ring bends the Sun's light inward and dims it. The air at the ground bends it most, by 1.2°,
and reddens it most, on a path through some eighty times the air overhead; its light crosses to the far side of the
shadow as a deep red. From 10 km up the light lands near the umbra's edge, brighter and less red.

Added up, that leaves a hundred thousandth of the sunlight's green at the shadow's axis, some seventeen stops down,
and five stops more towards the umbra's edge (<Fig id="umbra-light" />). Where the Moon sits in that light is one number
sternzeit gives per moment, `eclipse.lunar(jde).axisOffsetKm`, the distance of the Moon's center from the shadow's
axis. The disc reaches 1,737 km either side of it, so its two edges can lie stops apart.

<UmbraLight />

Sunlight that grazes the ozone of the stratosphere, 20 to 30 km up, loses its orange there and comes out turquoise,
and some photographs show that as a band just inside the umbra's edge. In the model it stays a tint: the Sun's half
degree spreads each layer's light over some 3,600 km of the shadow, and the red of the lower air floods the ozone's
blue.

A model of the umbra is only as good as its match with a real eclipse. Theodore Yapo and Barbara Cutler photographed
the one of February 21, 2008 for exactly that: raw frames through a telescope, at known exposures, decoded without
the camera's white balance <Cite id="yapo2009" />. With the exposure known, the pass can be held to it instead of
metering for itself, and at the photographs' own exposure nachtgestirn's Moon comes out within half a stop of them,
in totality as in the partial phase (<Fig id="photo-eclipse" />). The copper's red against its green is the same in
three of the four frames, and a quarter redder in the photograph early in totality; towards the shadow's edge both
turn pale, not turquoise.

<EclipsePhotos />

The air is dunstkreis' atmosphere model, traced on the CPU. Adding it up takes some 200 ms, once per eclipse, and
gives a table of 64 colors from the shadow's axis to the penumbra's edge, `refractedSunlight`, which the pass reads
per point of the surface:

```ts
import { refractedSunlight } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";
import { eclipse } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Once per eclipse: the shadow's size from sternzeit, the air from your
// atmosphere model, `deflection` and `transmittance` of a ray grazing
// Earth at a height in km, as dunstkreis traces them.
const shadow = eclipse.lunar(jde);
const refracted = refractedSunlight({
    deflection, transmittance,
    umbraKm: shadow.umbraRadiusKm,
    penumbraKm: shadow.penumbraRadiusKm,
    distanceKm: moon.distance(jde),
    sunAngularRadius: 0.00465,
});
```

What no model of clear air knows is this year's dust. The dust a volcano leaves in the stratosphere darkens the
eclipses after it, which the <Term of="Danjon scale">Danjon scale</Term> grades <Cite id="espenak2009" />, so the
brightness stays a factor the renderer sets, `umbraLight`. And a renderer that cannot spare the geometry per point can
color the disc from the eclipse's single phase instead, as sternzeit's chapter suggests and osgHimmel did
<Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />, with `typicalUmbra` as a hand-set copper. It gives up the soft edge sweeping across
the relief.

## Through the air

The Moon's light crosses the same air as the Sun's, and the same two things happen to it: the air takes some of it
away, the blue first, and adds its own light in front. The first is a <Term of="Transmittance">transmittance</Term>
along the line of sight, and it makes the rising Moon dim and orange. Near the horizon it changes fast: at moonrise
the lower limb lets a third as much green through as the upper, and none of the blue, so the pass takes it per ray.
The second it leaves to the sky drawn behind it, whose light it adds on top of the disc. That is the whole secret of
the Moon by day: it is as bright as at night, the sky in front of it is brighter still, and a renderer that draws
both in physical units gets the pale daytime Moon for free.

The air also bends the light, the <Term of="Atmospheric refraction">refraction</Term>, more the lower it comes in. On
the horizon it lifts the Moon's lower limb some 6 arcminutes more than its upper, and the rising Moon shows squeezed to
four fifths of its height. The pass bends every ray the way dunstkreis bends them for the Sun's disc: `refractionTable`
asks any refraction you hand it how far the air bends a ray, and what it lets through, at 32 altitudes across the disc.

```ts
import { refractionTable } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";
import { airTransmittance, refractionAngle } from "@himmelszelt/dunstkreis";

// How far the air bends a ray seen at an apparent altitude, and what it
// lets through, both from dunstkreis' model and the observer's height.
const refraction = refractionTable(
    direction, angularDiameter,
    (altitude) => refractionAngle(model, heightM, altitude),
    (altitude) => airTransmittance(model, heightM, altitude),
);
pass.update({ direction, angularDiameter, refraction });
```

On the horizon that adds up to a Moon at a tenth of its light, lifted by about its own width
(<Fig id="rising" />). Raise the observer, and the horizon sinks below the level, so a ray grazing it dips deeper into
the air and crosses it twice: from the space station's orbit the setting Moon sinks into the bright rim of the air
squeezed to a quarter of its height, as astronauts photograph it.

<Rising />

The air does not hold still either. Its turbulence makes the image wobble and boil, the
<Term of="Seeing">seeing</Term>: some half an <Term of="Arcsecond">arcsecond</Term> to an arcsecond overhead on a fair
night, and more the more air the light crosses <Cite id="fried1966" /> <Cite id="sarazin1990" />. The pass scales it
by the same air it dims the Moon by, so a Moon on the horizon, seen through some forty times the air overhead,
trembles most, and one seen from a mountain less. The air also bends blue a little more than red, and lifts the blue
image over the red by a hundredth of the refraction, tens of arcseconds on the horizon <Cite id="edlen1966" />
<Cite id="filippenko1982" /> (<Fig id="shimmer" />). `shimmer` sets the seeing and the clock. It is a camera's view
of the air, one short exposure at a time; the eye sees the Moon's limb tremble the same way on a warm night.

## The Moon tonight

Everything above comes together over a sky: the Moon at the chosen moment and place over dunstkreis' sky, through its
air, lit by the Sun and by Earth, in Earth's shadow when there is an eclipse, and exposed as a camera would
(<Fig id="moon" />). The line under the figure says what the frame cost, the astronomy on the CPU included.

Three things in that picture are the sky's, not the disc's. The first is moonlight: the Moon lights the air as the Sun
does, at a four hundred thousandth of the light, some 0.3 lux at full Moon, which `moonIlluminance` gives. The sky by
moonlight is the day's sky at that share, blue, with the same <Term of="Aureole">aureole</Term> of haze around the disc,
and dunstkreis draws it when its sky pass is made with `moonlight`. The second is glow. What surrounds a bright Moon and
is not haze is made in the camera or the eye, <Term of="Veiling glare">veiling glare</Term> and bloom; helldunkel adds
those to the finished image, where they belong, and the pass draws none.

The third is the exposure. The pass takes an <Term of="EV100">EV100</Term>, as dunstkreis' sky pass does, and the two
agree, since both start from the same sunlight above the air. A full Moon exposes around 14, like a sunny day: the
photographer's "looney 11", f/11 at the film's speed. That is why a night sky metered for the stars burns the Moon out
to a white disc. `moonEV100` meters on the lit part, and the figure takes that or the sky's exposure, whichever is
brighter.

That is the camera's night, the day's sky a million times dimmer and still blue. The eye sees it differently: below
some 4 cd/m² its <Term of="Scotopic vision">rods</Term> take over from the cones, which see color, and by moonlight
the rods see alone. Colors fade, red goes dark first, and what is left looks blue, which painters have always given
the night. The pass' `nightVision`, helldunkel's blue shift after Jensen and colleagues <Cite id="jensen2001" />,
turns the composed light into that before it is exposed; the disc itself, far brighter than what the rods handle,
keeps its color. Turn the exposure off auto in the figure, take it down to −3 and zoom out: the night around the full
Moon is blue either way, the camera's blue or the eye's.

The frame a renderer writes is the quick start with the sky behind it:

```ts
import { createSkyPass } from "@himmelszelt/dunstkreis";
import { moonEV100, moonIlluminance } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";

const sky = await createSkyPass(device, { ...skyOptions, moonlight: true });
// Per frame: the sky lit by the Sun and the Moon, then the Moon over it,
// both at one exposure.
const ev100 = moonEV100(phaseAngle, illuminatedFraction);
sky.update({
    sunDirection, moonDirection: direction,
    moonIlluminance: moonIlluminance(phaseAngle, distanceKm), ev100,
});
sky.encode(encoder, skyTexture);
// The quick start's inputs, the earthshine, the refraction table and, in an
// eclipse, Earth's shadow, else null.
pass.update({ earthshine, refraction, eclipse, ev100 });
pass.encode(encoder, target, { background: skyTexture });
```

The pass has two ways to hand over its Moon. By default it writes the disc alone into an rgba16float target, linear,
its coverage in alpha, for a renderer that blends the Moon into its own HDR frame and tone maps the whole at the end.
With `background` it composes the Moon over a sky in cd/m² itself, and with `toneMap` it applies one of helldunkel's
curves, so the result shows on an 8-bit canvas as it is, as in the figures here. What a frame does not need stays out
of its shader: the eclipse until the Moon touches Earth's penumbra, the air without a table, earthshine where none is
asked for.

The same pass draws more than the Moon in a sky. Zoomed in and out of the air, it is the globe at the top, which saves
its view as a PNG, a print of the Moon tonight. With `earthDirection` the viewer need not be on Earth: the Moon from
orbit, or from Mars, with Earth's glow on the side that faces Earth. sternzeit's [month of
Moons](../sternzeit/#a-month-of-moons) with these discs in place of its drawn ones is a moon calendar with Crisium in
its right place. And with another pair of maps, the same law draws any other dusty, airless ball, Mercury or Ceres.

## Cheap and full

A Moon in a sky is a disc of a dozen to a few hundred pixels, and needs little of the above. So the pass switches its
machinery on by the disc's size on screen. Under 128 pixels across it casts no shadows, `shadowsFrom`; under 1024 it
shades the relief on a smooth sphere instead of displacing it, `displacementFrom`. Each threshold is a number of
pixels, 0 for always and Infinity for never. The maps follow [the same size](#maps-and-relief), picked by
`mapWidthFor`.

One ray per pixel does for most of the disc, `samples`, since the maps and the shadow map are filtered over the pixel's
footprint. Along the terminator, where a crater rim turns from lit to dark within a pixel, and along a displaced limb,
the pass takes four, `terminatorSamples`. The rest is a choice: `typicalUmbra` in place of the integral, no shimmer, and
`compress`, which stores the color map as BC1 where the GPU has "texture-compression-bc", at an eighth of its memory,
for colors a hundredth of a stop off. The maps are read anisotropically, so the poles and the limb come out as sharp as
the center; `filter` switches the cheaper filters on.

Everything on, with the next finer maps, costs a disc up to four times the time, still under a millisecond, and four
times the memory. What the defaults leave out shows on about one in a hundred of the disc's pixels, most of them
crater rims along the terminator drawn a pixel off, where the relief is not displaced (<Fig id="budget" />).

<Budget />

Which of it a renderer needs depends on how large it draws the Moon. In a game's sky 60° tall on a screen 1080 px tall,
the disc is some nine pixels across, and the 512 maps without shadows are all it shows; through a 600 mm lens on a
full frame camera it is some 490 pixels, and the shadows along the terminator span several of them; filling a 4K
screen, it needs the site's 8K maps and the displaced limb. Past some 2,600 pixels even those give less than a texel to
a pixel, and the roughness fills in below them. Set the disc's size, and the switches follow the package's own
thresholds, each with its reason; change them, and the costs and the code follow too (<Fig id="configurator" />).

## The Eastern Sea

One last look, at the Moon's most striking face, which Earth only ever sees edge on. On the western limb, turned into
view when the libration is kind, lies Mare Orientale, the Eastern Sea. It got its name while east on the Moon was
still the sky's east, before the IAU turned east and west round for the astronauts in 1961. It is the youngest of the
great basins, younger than Imbrium's 3.85 billion years: three rings of mountains, the outer one, Montes Cordillera,
some 960 km across, around a dark sea of lava a third as wide. From Earth its rings are lines along the limb. Turned
to face us, they are a target (<Fig id="orientale" />). The pass shows any place on the Moon face on the way the
libration would, with the place's longitude and latitude in the libration's stead; Orientale's are the IAU's:

```ts
import { moonOrientation } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";

// Mare Orientale's middle, selenographic, east positive, in degrees.
const libration = { longitude: -94.67, latitude: -19.87 };
const orientation = moonOrientation({
    direction, libration, positionAngleOfAxis, parallacticAngle,
});
pass.update({ orientation });
```

## The people behind the numbers

Bruce Hapke worked out how a powdered surface reflects light in 1963 and improved its scattering term in 1966
<Cite id="hapke1963" /> <Cite id="hapke1966" />, added the coherent backscatter of light between grains in 2002
<Cite id="hapke2002" />, and gathered half a century of it in his book of 2012 <Cite id="hapke2012" />; the full
Moon's flatness and the opposition surge are his. Hiroyuki Sato and his colleagues on the orbiter's camera team fitted
that model to the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's images of the whole Moon in 2014 <Cite id="sato2014" />, the
parameters the pass shades with. Hugh Kieffer and Thomas Stone built the Robotic Lunar Observatory for the US
Geological Survey and measured the Moon's light night after night for years <Cite id="kieffer2005" />, the curve every
law here is held against.

Michael Oren and Shree Nayar gave rendering its rough surface in 1994 <Cite id="oren1994" />. Henrik Wann Jensen and
his colleagues put Hapke's law, with earthshine and the night sky's other lights, into a renderer in 2001
<Cite id="jensen2001" />, the paper osgHimmel's Moon followed. Hendrik van de Hulst's phase function of a scattering
sphere, from his book on multiple scattering of 1980 <Cite id="vandehulst1980" />, is where the earthshine's
dependence on Earth's phase comes from.

František Link, at the Ondřejov observatory, worked out from the 1930s on how Earth's air lights its own shadow, ring
by ring, and gathered it in his book of 1969 <Cite id="link1969" />; the umbra's table is his integral over
dunstkreis' air. Theodore Yapo and Barbara Cutler traced photons through that air for their eclipse renderings of 2009
<Cite id="yapo2009" />, the reference the thesis' hand-set colors were fitted against. David Fried described in 1966
how turbulence limits what a telescope resolves, the parameter the shimmer takes its seeing from
<Cite id="fried1966" />. The maps are Ernie Wright's, of NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio, who assembled the CGI
Moon Kit in 2019 and remade its color map in December 2025 <Cite id="svs4720" />.
