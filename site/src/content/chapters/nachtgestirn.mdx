Look at a full Moon and it is a coin, not a ball: as bright at its very edge as in its middle, with no shading to round
it off. A sphere lit from behind the viewer should darken towards its rim, and every planet a renderer shades with the
usual cosine does. The Moon does not, because its surface is dust: light that reaches a grain at a slant leaves it at
the same slant, through the same thin layer of other grains, and the two slants cancel. nachtgestirn draws the Moon that
way: its disc along every pixel's ray, shaded by Bruce Hapke's model of a powdered surface in its modern form
<Cite id="hapke2012" />, with the parameters Sato and colleagues fitted to the Moon <Cite id="sato2014" />, over NASA's
maps of its color and its relief <Cite id="svs4720" />, lit by the Sun and by <Term of="Earthshine">earthshine</Term>,
and darkened and reddened in Earth's shadow during a lunar eclipse. Where the Moon is, how large, how it is turned and
where the Sun is from it come from [sternzeit](../sternzeit/); the sky it is drawn over, and the air in front of it,
from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/). osgHimmel did the same with a cube map and a few hand-set intensities
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />; here the light is in lux and the disc in cd/m², so the Moon exposes like the sky around
it. The chapter keeps to what a renderer needs; for the Moon itself, its orbit, its tides, its light and its soil,
explored at length in interactive figures, Bartosz Ciechanowski's page on it is the one to read
<Cite id="ciechanowski2024" />.

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, one WebGPU compute pass, no dependency on any other package at runtime. It takes
directions, angles and lux, draws into a texture you own over a background in cd/m², or alone with its coverage in
alpha, and tone maps or stays linear, as dunstkreis' sky does. What it costs is on the line under <Fig id="moon" />:
a fraction of a millisecond, most of it the astronomy on the CPU.

The Moon below is that pass over dunstkreis' sky, the view kept on the Moon and zoomed in on it: drag to let go,
scroll to zoom out to the sky around it, and set the moment and the place under it, which every figure of the chapter
follows.

Four lines are the whole setup: the maps once, the pass once, and per frame the astronomy as sternzeit computes it,

[the face we see](#the-face-we-see).

```ts
import { createMoonPass, loadMoonMaps, MOON_MAPS } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";

const maps = await loadMoonMaps(device, MOON_MAPS.medium);
const pass = await createMoonPass(device, { format: "rgba8unorm", maps });
// Per frame: where it is, how large, where the Sun is from it, how it is turned.
pass.update({ direction, angularDiameter, sunDirection, orientation, ev100: 14 });
pass.encode(encoder, target, { background: skyInCandelaPerSquareMeter });
```

## Sunlight on dust

Hold a ball up to the Sun and its edge goes dark: the light falls on the rim at a slant, and a surface that scatters
light evenly in every direction, a Lambert surface, the law a renderer reaches for first, reflects a slant of 80° at a
sixth of what it reflects face on. The Moon's surface is a powder, and a powder is lit and seen through the same loose
layer of grains: what a slant costs on the way in, it gives back on the way out. The
<Term of="Lommel-Seeliger law">Lommel-Seeliger law</Term> says it in one fraction, the cosine of the light's angle over
the sum of the cosines of the light's and the viewer's angles, and at full Moon, where the two angles are the same
everywhere on the disc, the fraction is a half at the center and a half at the rim. The Moon is flat. Seen from the
side, with the light that falls on a patch of surface spread over the directions it leaves in (<Fig id="lobes" />), a
Lambert patch sends the most straight up whatever the Sun does, and a powder sends the most straight back at the Sun.

<Lobes />

Nobody has shown this better than Bartosz Ciechanowski. On
[his page on the Moon](https://ciechanow.ski/moon/#lunar-brightness) a ball of lunar soil flattens into a disc as you
swing the light behind you, a field of grains hides its shadows one by one, and two grains send their light back in
step <Cite id="ciechanowski2024" />. Go and play with it; this chapter only takes what it shows to a renderer.

Hapke's lunar law adds what a powder does near opposition, with the Sun behind the viewer: every grain hides its own
shadow, and the surface brightens by the <Term of="Opposition surge">opposition surge</Term>, a factor 2 at a
<Term of="Phase angle">phase angle</Term> of 0 that is gone by 90° <Cite id="hapke1963" />, and a little forward
scattering, which keeps a thin crescent a rim of light towards the Sun. Light scattered between grains adds to the surge
as well, in step on its way back, the coherent backscatter Hapke added to his law decades later <Cite id="hapke2002" />;
the law here lumps both into one surge, fitted to the Moon's light. Jensen and colleagues brought the law into rendering
<Cite id="jensen2001" />; osgHimmel used it with a scattering term from Hapke's second paper <Cite id="hapke1966" />,
which makes the quarters a stop too bright, and nachtgestirn keeps Jensen's for its law of 1963. The law is scaled so
that a point seen and lit straight on reflects its <Term of="Normal albedo">normal albedo</Term> as a Lambert surface
would: the full Moon, of <Term of="Geometric albedo">geometric albedo</Term> 0.12 <Cite id="nssdc" />, in the 128,000
lux of the Sun above the air, is a flat disc of some 4,900 cd/m², a sunlit rock, to be exposed like a landscape at noon.

By the Moon's phase law a quarter Moon gives a tenth of that, not half <Cite id="cox2000" />: the
<Term of="Illuminated fraction">illuminated fraction</Term> is half, but every crater throws a shadow, and the surge is
gone. The figures' light meter follows that law and exposes the lit part a stop brighter than mid gray, as a
photographer does. We never see the surge in full: the Moon passes above or below Earth's shadow most months, and the
phase angle at full Moon stays a few degrees.

Hapke kept working on the law for half a century. Its modern form <Cite id="hapke2012" /> gives the grains a phase
function of two lobes, one back towards the Sun and one onwards, adds the light scattered many times between them, and
shades the surface by the shadows of its slopes, a mean slope of some 24° <Cite id="hapke1984" />. Sato and colleagues
fitted it to some 66,000 images of the Moon from the wide angle camera of the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, tile by tile
<Cite id="sato2014" />. nachtgestirn draws the Moon by it, with the near side's median parameters at 566 nm, no slower
than by the old law, which it keeps as a choice.

The phase law is measured for the whole disc, so that is where a reflectance law can be checked (<Fig id="phases" />).
The best measurement is the US Geological Survey's Robotic Lunar Observatory, over a thousand nights fitted to a percent
between phase angles of 1.55° and 97° <Cite id="kieffer2005" />: its surge is steeper than Allen's law has it, and its
quarter Moon gives a fifteenth of the full. A Lambert sphere gives two thirds of a flat disc's light at full and a third
of its own full at a quarter, and its half Moon comes out three times too bright. Hapke's law of 1963 comes out a
quarter to two fifths of a stop too bright, its surge too broad. His model of 2012 follows the measurement within a
fifth of a stop all the way, from full to 97°; beyond, where only Allen's law is left, its crescents fall a stop under
by 150°. None of this is a matter of taste: a renderer with the wrong law is off by stops.

<Phases />
<Controls height={false} />

On the disc (<Fig id="laws" />), Lambert's law, the cosine of the Sun's angle and nothing else, the term every renderer
starts from, rounds the full Moon into a ball with a dark rim and lets a half Moon fade into its terminator. Oren and
Nayar's rough surface <Cite id="oren1994" />, the law a renderer reaches for when Lambert's limb looks wrong, brightens
the limb towards full Moon with the shadows its facets throw on each other, and gets the full Moon nearly flat; it has
no surge, its half Moon comes out some two and a half times too bright, and it still fades into the terminator. Both of
Hapke's keep the full Moon flat to its edge. At the quarters they part: the law of 1963 stays bright up to the
terminator, the model of 2012, its slopes shading each other, fades into it. Switch the discs to the day and the Moon
does not change, the sky behind it does: the night side vanishes into a sky as bright as itself, and the lit part shows
pale, the daytime Moon, with nothing done to the Moon.

## The face we see

The Moon turns once per orbit, so it shows us one face, almost: the <Term of="Libration">libration</Term> nods it a
few degrees each way over a month, and the face is turned on the sky as well, by the
<Term of="Position angle of axis">position angle of its axis</Term> from the sky's north, and from the sky's north to
your own up by the <Term of="Parallactic angle">parallactic angle</Term>, both from sternzeit. nachtgestirn folds the
three into one rotation, from your frame into the Moon's own, and asks the maps where every point of the disc is in
<Term of="Selenographic coordinates">selenographic coordinates</Term>: longitude east positive, latitude north. East
on the Moon is the side where the Sun rises on it, the side of Mare Crisium, the lone dark oval which stands on the
right of the disc with north up as seen from Earth's northern hemisphere, and which sternzeit's own Moon, drawn with a
grid rather than a map, puts in the same place.

<Fig id="moon-face" /> lays the two over each other: sternzeit's view of the Moon, [its figure](../sternzeit/#moon-view), with its grid, axis and arrow to the
Sun, and behind it the Moon from the maps, turned by the same three angles. The grid's center is the point of the
Moon's face that looks at you, its meridian of 0° the one the map is centered on, and both move together as the
libration nods the face. The view stays locked to the Moon, seen from Earth's center with its north up and no air in
front; the Moon in the sky, through the air, is at the top of the chapter (<Fig id="moon" />) and
[through the air](#through-the-air). The libration is the month's; over a night the Moon's face turns a
little more, by up to a degree, as Earth's turning carries you from one side of its center to the other, which neither
sternzeit nor the figure follow yet.

## Maps and relief

Every Moon in a renderer is a photograph wrapped around a sphere, and which photograph matters. nachtgestirn's maps are
NASA's, from the CGI Moon Kit of its Scientific Visualization Studio <Cite id="svs4720" /> (<Fig id="maps" />): the
color from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's wide angle camera, over 100,000 images, each taken with the Sun high over
it, into one mosaic, remade in December 2025 with more of the source's color and range, and the heights from the
orbiter's laser altimeter, which timed a laser pulse to the ground billions of times. Both are laid out with 0°
longitude in the middle and east to the right, so a point's longitude and latitude read straight off them, with no
mirroring, which osgHimmel's cube map needed <Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />. A rerunnable script makes both from the kit,
the heights from the altimeter's 64 texels per degree, each 4096, 1024 and 512 texels around, all shipped with the
package. A disc shows a map π times its width in pixels at one texel per pixel, so the 230 KB set does for a disc up to
some 160 pixels across, the 840 KB one to some 330, and the figures load the 11.5 MB one only once you zoom in further,
or when you ask for it.

<Maps />
<Controls height={false} />

The color map was made with the Sun high over every part of it, which is what makes it a map and not a picture: it
holds no shadows, so a renderer has to make its own. The thesis' Moon blended a normal map into the sphere's normals
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />, as most renderers do. A normal tilts the shading, it casts nothing, and along the
<Term of="Terminator">terminator</Term>, where the Sun stands a few degrees over the surface, the shadows are what the
eye sees: crater floors in darkness under lit rims, and peaks beyond the terminator lit before the plain around them.
nachtgestirn ships the heights instead, 16 bits a texel in two bytes of a WebP, and derives the slopes from them on the
GPU as the maps are loaded. Heights are a surface the pass can march through. A normal map is not. LOLA measures them
from a sphere of the Moon's mean radius, 1737.4 km, up and down: from 9.2 km below it, in the South Pole-Aitken basin,
to 10.8 km above <Cite id="smith2010" />, so the relief both stands on the smooth sphere and sinks into it, and a march runs through the shell
between.

<Fig id="surface" /> shows the three stages on a close-up of the terminator, and a fourth, the relief displaced. The shadows come from a march: from every
point the pass walks towards the Sun along the great circle, 24 steps, each a quarter farther than the last, from three
quarters of a texel out to some 450 km on the 4K map, as far as a mountain's shadow runs with the Sun a degree up, and
keeps the highest the terrain rises above the point's horizontal. What is left of the Sun is the share of its half
degree above that horizon; the terrain is read from a coarser level of the map the farther the step, so no peak falls
between two. Near the rim or peak that casts it, that horizon changes fast from point to point and the shadow's edge is
sharp; farther off slowly, and the penumbra widens, the contact hardening a renderer of soft shadows looks for, here by
the Sun's own size. The march runs only where the Sun stands less than 25° over the surface, as no crater rim hides a
Sun higher than that, and is skipped elsewhere, which is most of the disc. It runs once, not for every pixel and frame:
into a shadow map of that band around the terminator, as fine as the disc on screen needs, kept while the Sun and the
view stay where they are. The Sun crosses the Moon's sky at half a degree an hour, so in a sky one map outlives many
frames, and a disc under 128 pixels, the Moon as a sky shows it, takes no shadows at all.

Shading a smooth sphere by the relief's slopes, what a normal map does, holds while the terrain faces you. Near the limb
it fails: you look across the terrain at a slant, the near rims hide the floors behind them, and the limb itself is the
profile of the mountains standing on it, up to 11 km over the mean radius, a few pixels on a disc that fills a
screen. Shaded, a slope turned away from you shows anyway, as a black dash. From a disc of 1024 pixels on, the pass
marches every ray near the limb through the heights and shades the terrain where the ray meets it, so the near rims hide
what lies behind them and the limb shows its mountains (<Fig id="globe" />, and the last close-up of <Fig id="surface" />).

The Sun's half degree is what makes the shadows soft, and the share of its disc costs the march nothing it would not
do anyway. What it buys is small: a penumbra is a hundredth of the shadow's length, 0.85 km for a shadow 90 km long, a
3 km rim with the Sun 2° up, and 0.85 km is a pixel of a disc 4096 pixels across. Smaller discs show it only on the
longest shadows, if at all, so the pass casts with a point Sun below `softShadowsFrom`, 4096 pixels by default, and the
shadow map holds one less thing that changes. <Fig id="soft-shadows" /> puts the two side by side, far closer in than
any sky shows the Moon.

<SoftShadows />
<Controls height={false} />

What the relief and its shadows give is sharper than a pixel, and one sample per pixel could alias on it. It
hardly does: the maps are read over the whole pixel's footprint, and the shadows from their
map, filtered too, so one sample through each pixel's center comes close to 64. The pass takes one, and four along the
terminator, where a crater rim still turns from lit to dark within a pixel, and along a displaced limb, spread by a
low-discrepancy sequence and their light averaged as a sensor does; `samples` and `terminatorSamples` set both. One trap
lies in the plain geometry: the angle between a ray and the Moon's center, taken from their dot product, which sits
within a hundred-thousandth of 1, keeps only some five digits in the GPU's 32-bit floats, steps of a third of a percent
of the radius, stairs a few pixels wide on a large disc. The pass takes it from the two directions' difference instead,
which keeps all seven. Towards the poles the
map's parallels crowd the texels together east to west, and at the limb the slant stretches a ray's footprint along the
line of sight up to fifty times; a plain mip level takes the longer of the two and blurs what the other axis still
resolves, so the maps are filtered anisotropically over the footprint's two axes (<Fig id="sampling" />), and the slopes are averaged in the
mips as slopes, not derived again. The poles come out as sharp as the center. Step the moment a day at a time in
<Fig id="moon" /> and the face stays put while the terminator sweeps across it, Crisium on the right throughout,
nodding in and out with the libration. With the shadows off the terminator goes soft, with the relief off smooth; with
the map off the Moon is a plain disc of its albedo, the coin of the opening.

## The Moon's color

The color map is balanced to look right, not to measure, and nearly neutral, and most renderers throw even that away
and wrap a gray map around the sphere. Gray is wrong twice (<Fig id="color" />). The Moon is not gray: it is redder than
the sunlight it reflects, a <Term of="Color index">color index</Term> B-V of 0.92 against the Sun's 0.65
<Cite id="cox2000" />, a warm gray outside the air, as the astronauts photographed it, and a gray disc over a sky drawn
in physical units sits colder than the sky around it. And the map's small differences in hue are real: the
titanium-rich basalt of Mare Tranquillitatis is a little bluer than the highlands, which a gray map flattens into the
same shade of dark. nachtgestirn scales the map to <Term of="Normal albedo">normal albedo</Term> by the near side's mean
and tints it towards the Moon's own color, keeping its variations; `mapColor` reads it as published, or as gray, for a
renderer that wants the paper-white Moon of the posters.

## Near and far

The Moon's orbit is not a circle, and its size in the sky says so: at <Term of="Perigee">perigee</Term> the disc is
14% wider than at <Term of="Apogee">apogee</Term>, 33.5 arcminutes against 29.4, and a full Moon near perigee, the
supermoon of the news, gives a third more light than one near apogee. The pass takes the diameter as a plain angle,
`moon.topocentricAngularDiameter` on the site, so the size follows the orbit without the pass knowing of one
(<Fig id="near-far" />). The second number in that name matters too: from where you stand, not from Earth's center,
the Moon overhead is an Earth radius nearer than on the horizon, and almost 2% wider. The rising Moon is the smaller
one, whatever it looks like: the <Term of="Moon illusion">Moon illusion</Term> is the mind's, and a renderer that
draws the Moon larger on the horizon is drawing the illusion, not the sky.

## Earthshine

The night side of a thin crescent is not black: it glows with sunlight Earth reflects onto it, and the glow is
strongest when the crescent is thinnest, because the Moon then sees an almost full Earth. nachtgestirn takes
earthshine as what it is, an illuminance at the Moon in lux: Earth as a Lambert sphere of its geometric albedo of
0.367 <Cite id="nssdc" />, at its phase as the Moon sees it, which is the Moon's own phase turned around
<Cite id="vandehulst1980" />. A full Earth lights the Moon with some 13 lux, a ten thousandth of the Sun's 128,000, and
about fifty times what a full Moon gives Earth back: Earth is four times wider and three times brighter. The night
side then shines at half a candela per square meter, thirteen stops below the crescent's sunlit part, which is why a photograph exposed for the crescent shows it faintly and the eye, which adapts across
the disc, sees it plainly. The light comes from Earth's center, which the Moon sees at most a degree from where we
look from, so the night side is lit almost face on, flat like the day side at full Moon.

Set <Fig id="moon" /> to March 21, 2026 at 19:30 local time in Berlin, a crescent of a tenth 21° up in the west at the
end of twilight: the night side shows the maria faintly in the earthshine, and lowering the exposure by a few stops
brings them out, as a long exposure does.

## Earth's shadow

Twice or so a year the full Moon passes through Earth's shadow, and the shadow has two parts: the
<Term of="Umbra">penumbra</Term>, where Earth covers the Sun's disc only partly, and the umbra, where it
covers it whole. nachtgestirn computes both per point of the Moon's surface, from the point's own view: how much of the
Sun's disc Earth's disc hides is the area two circles share, which is a smooth ramp across the penumbra, some 8,200 km
wide at the Moon's distance, and nothing of the Sun is left in the umbra, 4,600 km in radius. The geometry comes from
sternzeit's `eclipse.lunar`, the umbra's and penumbra's radii and the Moon's distance; the Moon's place in the shadow
has to be taken from Earth's center, not from where you stand, since the parallax of up to a degree would move the
Moon by 6,700 km, more than the umbra's radius.

The umbra is not black either. Earth's atmosphere bends sunlight around the planet into the shadow, the light of
every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once, and leaves the eclipsed Moon copper, some ten thousand times fainter than
full. How copper, and how bright, nachtgestirn does not set by hand: it integrates it. Seen from a point in the shadow,
Earth's dark disc is ringed by its air, and every ring of it, a quarter of a kilometer of height at a time around the
whole limb, bends the Sun's light inward by an angle that falls with the height, 1.2° at the ground and a tenth of that
20 km up, and dims it by what that grazing path lets through, a path through some eighty times the air overhead. The
point adds up every ring whose bent ray came from somewhere on the Sun's disc, after the theory František Link worked
out for the eclipses of his time <Cite id="link1969" />, and to the same end Yapo and Cutler traced photons through the
air for their eclipse renderings <Cite id="yapo2009" />, far from real time. Here the two functions of the height, the
bend and the transmittance, come from dunstkreis' atmosphere model, traced on the CPU, and the integral takes some
200 ms once per eclipse and comes out as a table of 64 colors from the shadow's axis to the penumbra's edge, which the
pass reads per point of the surface, `refractedSunlight`. Near sea level the air bends the light past the axis to the
shadow's far side, dimmed to a deep red by the long path; from 10 km up it lands near the umbra's edge, brighter and
less red. At the axis the model leaves some sixteen stops below the sunlight, a hundred thousandth, and four stops more
at the umbra's edge, within what photographs of eclipses show; the turquoise rim some of them show just inside the edge,
where the light passed the ozone of the stratosphere, is faint in the model, a tint rather than a band. How bright an
eclipse comes out varies with the dust in the stratosphere, after a volcano above all, which the Danjon scale grades
<Cite id="espenak2009" />, and that stays a factor the renderer sets: no model of a clear atmosphere knows this year's
dust. The figure's light meter follows the shadow: as the umbra swallows the disc, the exposure opens up until the
copper shows, which is what the eye does too.

<Fig id="eclipse-row" /> runs the Moon through the next eclipse from the umbra's first contact to its last, each
disc metered on its own, or all at the uneclipsed Moon's exposure, where the copper is barely there:
the shadow's edge is a soft ramp, the Sun's disc setting behind Earth as seen from the Moon, and the copper inside it
is twelve to sixteen stops below the sunlight, deeper towards the axis. The curves under the discs are the table, the light along a line out from the
axis, as the pass reads it. The timeline above the row holds the eclipses of twelve years either side of the chosen
moment, a bar each as tall as the eclipse is deep, the total ones in the accent: a click goes to one, and the buttons
on the big Moon step to the one before or after; the strip itself moves along only for an eclipse at its ends. A renderer that cannot spare the geometry per point can read the
shadow's color from the eclipse's single phase instead, as sternzeit's chapter suggests and osgHimmel did
<Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />; what it gives up is the soft edge sweeping across the relief.

## Through the air

The Moon's light crosses the same air as the Sun's, and the same two things happen to it: the air takes some of it
away, more of the blue, and adds its own light in front. nachtgestirn takes the first as a
<Term of="Transmittance">transmittance</Term> per channel along the line of sight, so the rising Moon comes out dim and
orange. Near the horizon a ray half a degree lower crosses far more air: at moonrise the lower limb lets a third as much

The air also bends the Moon's light, and more the lower it comes in: on the horizon it lifts the lower limb some 6′
more than the upper, and the rising Moon shows squeezed to four fifths of its height. The pass bends every ray as
dunstkreis bends them for the sun disc: `refractionTable` asks any refraction you hand it, the figure dunstkreis'
own, how far the air bends a ray at 32 apparent altitudes over where the disc shows, and the pass turns each ray down
by it before it meets the sphere. Seen from orbit, where a ray grazing the planet crosses the air twice and dips deep
into it, the same table squeezes the setting Moon to a quarter of its height, as astronauts photograph it.

The air does not hold still either. Its turbulence moves the image about and makes it boil, the
<Term of="Seeing">seeing</Term>, some half an arcsecond to an arcsecond overhead on a fair night, and more the more
air the light crosses <Cite id="fried1966" /> <Cite id="sarazin1990" />. The pass takes that air from the same optical depth it dims the Moon by: the column towards the
Moon over the zenith's from sea level, so a Moon on the horizon looks through some forty columns and one seen from a
mountain through fewer. The image moves with the square root of the column, the turbulence's cells shrink with it, and
the air's dispersion, which bends blue more than red, lifts the blue image over the red by a hundredth of the
refraction, tens of arcseconds on the horizon <Cite id="edlen1966" /> <Cite id="filippenko1982" /> (<Fig id="shimmer" />). `shimmer` sets the seeing and the clock, and
`motion` and `boiling` weigh the larger cells, which move patches of the image as a whole, and the smaller.

The Moon lights the air as the Sun does, at a four hundred thousandth of the light: some 0.3 lux above the air at full
Moon, which `moonIlluminance` gives. The sky by moonlight is the day's sky at that share, blue, with the aureole of the
haze around the disc, and dunstkreis draws it from a second sky-view table built from the Moon's direction when its
sky pass is made with `moonlight`. In <Fig id="moon" />, turn the exposure off auto, take it down to −3 and zoom out:
the night around the full Moon is blue.

The glow around a bright Moon that is not haze is the camera's or the eye's: veiling glare and bloom, which helldunkel
adds to the finished image, where they belong, and not the disc's, so the pass draws none.

That is the camera's blue, the day's sky a million times dimmer. The eye sees the night differently: below some
4 cd/m² its <Term of="Scotopic vision">rods</Term> take over from the cones, which see color, and by moonlight the
rods see alone. Colors fade, red goes dark first, and what is left looks blue, which painters have always given the
night. The pass' `nightVision`, helldunkel's blue shift after Jensen et al. <Cite id="jensen2001" />, turns the composed light into that before
it is exposed, as sternenheer's night sky shows. The disc itself, at 2,500 cd/m² far brighter than what the rods handle,
keeps its color.

The exposure is the renderer's: the pass takes an EV100 like dunstkreis' sky pass, and the two agree, since both
start from the same 128,000 lux. A full Moon exposes around 14 like a sunny day, the photographer's "looney 11" of
f/11 at the film's speed, which is why a night sky metered for the stars burns the Moon out to a white disc. The figure
meters on the Moon, or by day on the sky, whichever is brighter.

## The people behind the numbers

Bruce Hapke worked out the photometric function of a powdered surface in 1963 and improved its scattering term in 1966
<Cite id="hapke1963" /> <Cite id="hapke1966" />, and kept refining it for half a century, to the model of his book of
2012 <Cite id="hapke2012" />; the full Moon's flatness and the opposition surge are his. Henrik Wann Jensen and his
colleagues put that law, with earthshine and the night sky's other lights, into a renderer in 2001
<Cite id="jensen2001" />, the paper osgHimmel's Moon followed. Hendrik van de Hulst's phase function of a scattering
sphere, from his book on multiple scattering of 1980 <Cite id="vandehulst1980" />, is where the earthshine's dependence
on Earth's phase comes from. František Link, at the Ondřejov observatory, worked out from the 1930s on how Earth's air
lights its own shadow, ring by ring, and gathered it in his book of 1969 <Cite id="link1969" />; the umbra's table is
his integral over dunstkreis' air. Theodore Yapo and Barbara Cutler traced photons through that air for their eclipse
renderings of 2009 <Cite id="yapo2009" />, the reference the thesis' hand-set colors were fitted against. The maps are
Ernie Wright's, of NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio, who assembled the CGI Moon Kit in 2019 and remade its color
map in December 2025 <Cite id="svs4720" />.
