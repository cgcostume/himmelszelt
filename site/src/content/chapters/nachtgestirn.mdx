still to come.

Look at a full Moon and it is a coin, not a ball: as bright at its very edge as in its middle, with no shading to
round it off. A sphere lit from behind the viewer should darken towards its rim, and every planet a renderer shades
with the usual cosine does. The Moon does not, because its surface is dust: light that reaches a grain at a slant
leaves it at the same slant, through the same thin layer of other grains, and the two slants cancel. nachtgestirn
draws the Moon that way: its disc along every pixel's ray, shaded by the lunar reflectance law of Bruce Hapke
<Cite id="hapke1963" /> as Jensen and colleagues used it in their night sky model <Cite id="jensen2001" />, over NASA's
maps of its color and its relief <Cite id="svs4720" />, lit by the Sun and by <Term of="Earthshine">earthshine</Term>,
and darkened and reddened in Earth's shadow during a lunar eclipse. Where the Moon is, how large, how it is turned and
where the Sun is from it come from [sternzeit](../sternzeit/); the sky it is drawn over, and the air in front of it,
from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/). osgHimmel did the same with a cube map and a few hand-set intensities
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />; here the light is in lux and the disc in cd/m², so the Moon exposes like the sky
around it.

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, one WebGPU compute pass, no dependency on any other package at runtime. It takes
directions, angles and lux, draws into a texture you own over a background in cd/m², or alone with its coverage in
alpha, and tone maps or stays linear, as dunstkreis' sky does. What it costs is on the line under <Fig id="moon" />:
a fraction of a millisecond, most of it the astronomy on the CPU.

The Moon below is that pass over dunstkreis' sky, the view kept on the Moon and zoomed in on it: drag to let go,
scroll to zoom out to the sky around it, and set the moment and the place under it, which every figure of the chapter
follows.

Four lines are the whole setup: the maps once, the pass once, and per frame the astronomy as sternzeit computes it,
`moon.direction`, `moon.topocentricAngularDiameter`, `moon.sunDirection` and the orientation from
[the face we see](#the-face-we-see).

```ts
import { createMoonPass, loadMoonMaps, MOON_MAPS } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";

const maps = await loadMoonMaps(device, MOON_MAPS.medium);
const pass = await createMoonPass(device, { format: "rgba8unorm", maps });
// Per frame: where it is, how large, where the Sun is from it, how it is turned.
pass.update({ direction, angularDiameter, sunDirection, orientation, ev100: 14 });
pass.encode(encoder, target, { background: skyInCandelaPerSquareMeter });
```

## Sunlight on dust

Hold a ball up to the Sun and its edge goes dark: the light falls on the rim at a slant, and a surface that scatters
light evenly in every direction, a Lambert surface, the law a renderer reaches for first, reflects a slant of 80° at a
sixth of what it reflects face on. The Moon's surface is a powder, and a powder is lit and seen through the same loose
layer of grains: what a slant costs on the way in, it gives back on the way out. The
<Term of="Lommel-Seeliger law">Lommel-Seeliger law</Term> says it in one fraction, the cosine of the light's angle over
the sum of the cosines of the light's and the viewer's angles, and at full Moon, where the two angles are the same
everywhere on the disc, the fraction is a half at the center and a half at the rim. The Moon is flat.

Hapke's lunar law adds what a powder does near opposition, with the Sun behind the viewer: every grain hides its own
shadow, and the surface brightens by the <Term of="Opposition surge">opposition surge</Term>, a factor 2 at a
<Term of="Phase angle">phase angle</Term> of 0 that is gone by 90° <Cite id="hapke1963" />, and a little forward
scattering, which keeps a thin crescent a rim of light towards the Sun. Jensen and colleagues brought the law into
rendering <Cite id="jensen2001" />; osgHimmel used it with a scattering term from Hapke's second paper
<Cite id="hapke1966" />, which makes the quarters a stop too bright, and nachtgestirn keeps Jensen's. The law is
scaled so that a point seen and lit straight on reflects its <Term of="Normal albedo">normal albedo</Term> as a
Lambert surface would: the full Moon, of <Term of="Geometric albedo">geometric albedo</Term> 0.12
<Cite id="nssdc" />, in the 128,000 lux of the Sun above the air, is a flat disc of some 4,900 cd/m², a sunlit rock,
to be exposed like a landscape at noon. By the Moon's phase law a quarter Moon gives a tenth of that, not half
<Cite id="cox2000" />: the <Term of="Illuminated fraction">illuminated fraction</Term> is half, but every crater throws
a shadow, and the surge is gone. The figure's light meter follows that law and exposes the lit part a stop brighter
than mid gray, as a photographer does.

Switch the law to Lambert's in <Fig id="moon" /> at a full Moon, and the disc turns into a ball with a dark rim; switch
back, and it is a coin again. We never see the surge in full: the Moon passes above or below Earth's shadow most
months, and the phase angle at full Moon stays a few degrees. The one time the Moon stands exactly opposite the Sun is
when Earth's shadow falls on it.

## The face we see

The Moon turns once per orbit, so it shows us one face, almost: the <Term of="Libration">libration</Term> nods it a
few degrees each way over a month, and the face is turned on the sky as well, by the
<Term of="Position angle of axis">position angle of its axis</Term> from the sky's north, and from the sky's north to
your own up by the <Term of="Parallactic angle">parallactic angle</Term>, both from sternzeit. nachtgestirn folds the
three into one rotation, from your frame into the Moon's own, and asks the maps where every point of the disc is in
<Term of="Selenographic coordinates">selenographic coordinates</Term>: longitude east positive, latitude north. East
on the Moon is the side where the Sun rises on it, the side of Mare Crisium, the lone dark oval which stands on the
right of the disc with north up as seen from Earth's northern hemisphere, and which sternzeit's own Moon, drawn with a
grid rather than a map, puts in the same place.

<Fig id="moon-face" /> lays the two over each other: sternzeit's view of the Moon with its grid, axis and arrow to the
Sun, and behind it the Moon from the maps, turned by the same three angles. The grid's center is the point of the
Moon's face that looks at you, its meridian of 0° the one the map is centered on, and both move together as the
libration nods the face. Lock it to the Moon frame and the air goes: seen from Earth's center, with its north up, the
disc is the one the overlay has always drawn.

The maps are NASA's, from the CGI Moon Kit of its Scientific Visualization Studio <Cite id="svs4720" />: the color
from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's wide angle camera, over 100,000 images into one mosaic, remade in December 2025
with more of the source's color and range, and the relief from its laser altimeter. Both are laid out with 0° longitude
in the middle and east to the right, so a point's longitude and latitude read straight off them, with no mirroring, which
osgHimmel's cube map needed. A rerunnable script makes both from the kit, the heights from the altimeter's 64 texels
per degree, each 4096, 1024 and 512 texels around, all shipped with the package. A disc shows a map π times its width
in pixels at one texel per pixel, so the 230 KB set does for a disc up to some 160 pixels across, the 840 KB one to
some 330, and the figure loads the 11.5 MB one only once you zoom in further, or when you ask for it.

The color map is balanced to look right, not to measure, and nearly neutral, so the package scales it to normal
albedo by the near side's mean and tints it towards the Moon's own color, keeping its variations: the Moon is redder
than the sunlight it reflects, a <Term of="Color index">color index</Term> B-V of 0.92 against the Sun's 0.65
<Cite id="cox2000" />, which makes it a warm gray outside the air, as the astronauts photographed it, with the
titanium-rich Mare Tranquillitatis a little bluer than the rest.

The heights matter along the <Term of="Terminator">terminator</Term>, where the Sun stands low over the surface. The
pass turns them into the surface's slope for its shading, and marches from every point through them towards the Sun
for its shadows: the highest the terrain rises above the point's horizon against how much of the Sun's half degree
stands above that. Near the rim or peak that casts it, that height changes fast with the point, and the shadow's edge
is sharp; farther off slowly, and the penumbra widens, the contact hardening a renderer of soft shadows looks for,
here by the Sun's own size. Crater floors fill with shadow, and peaks beyond the terminator catch the light first.

Step the moment a day at a time in <Fig id="moon" /> and the face stays put while the terminator sweeps across it,
Crisium on the right throughout, nodding in and out with the libration. With the shadows off the terminator goes
soft, with the relief off smooth; with the map off the Moon is a plain disc of its albedo, the coin of the opening.

```ts
import { moonOrientation } from "@himmelszelt/nachtgestirn";
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, moon } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const time = fromDate(new Date());
const obs = { latitude, longitude };
const jde = julianEphemerisDay(time);
// From your frame into the Moon's own, east, north and up on the Moon.
const orientation = moonOrientation({
    direction: moon.direction(time, obs),
    libration: moon.librations(jde),
    positionAngleOfAxis: moon.positionAngleOfAxis(jde),
    parallacticAngle: moon.parallacticAngle(time, obs),
});
```

## Earthshine

The night side of a thin crescent is not black: it glows with sunlight Earth reflects onto it, and the glow is
strongest when the crescent is thinnest, because the Moon then sees an almost full Earth. nachtgestirn takes
earthshine as what it is, an illuminance at the Moon in lux: Earth as a Lambert sphere of its geometric albedo of
0.367 <Cite id="nssdc" />, at its phase as the Moon sees it, which is the Moon's own phase turned around
<Cite id="vandehulst1980" />. A full Earth lights the Moon with some 13 lux, a ten thousandth of the Sun's 128,000, and
about fifty times what a full Moon gives Earth back: Earth is four times wider and three times brighter. The night
side then shines at half a candela per square meter, thirteen stops below the crescent's sunlit part, which is why a photograph exposed for the crescent shows it faintly and the eye, which adapts across
the disc, sees it plainly. The light comes from Earth's center, which the Moon sees at most a degree from where we
look from, so the night side is lit almost face on, flat like the day side at full Moon.

Set <Fig id="moon" /> to March 21, 2026 at 19:30 local time in Berlin, a crescent of a tenth 21° up in the west at the
end of twilight: the night side shows the maria faintly in the earthshine, and lowering the exposure by a few stops
brings them out, as a long exposure does.

## Earth's shadow

Twice or so a year the full Moon passes through Earth's shadow, and the shadow has two parts: the
<Term of="Umbra">penumbra</Term>, where Earth covers the Sun's disc only partly, and the umbra, where it
covers it whole. nachtgestirn computes both per point of the Moon's surface, from the point's own view: how much of the
Sun's disc Earth's disc hides is the area two circles share, which is a smooth ramp across the penumbra, some 8,200 km
wide at the Moon's distance, and nothing of the Sun is left in the umbra, 4,600 km in radius. The geometry comes from
sternzeit's `eclipse.lunar`, the umbra's and penumbra's radii and the Moon's distance; the Moon's place in the shadow
has to be taken from Earth's center, not from where you stand, since the parallax of up to a degree would move the
Moon by 6,700 km, more than the umbra's radius.

The umbra is not black either. Earth's atmosphere bends sunlight around the planet into the shadow, the light of
every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once, and leaves the eclipsed Moon copper, some ten thousand times fainter than
full. Along the umbra's rim the light has passed the stratosphere, where ozone takes the orange out, and the rim
turns turquoise. How bright the umbra is varies from eclipse to eclipse with the dust in the stratosphere, after a
volcano above all, which the Danjon scale grades <Cite id="espenak2009" />; nachtgestirn models it as a tenth of a
thousandth of the sunlight at the shadow's axis, thirty times that at its edge, and lets a renderer scale it. The
figure's light meter follows the shadow: as the umbra swallows the disc, the exposure opens up until the copper
shows, which is what the eye does too.

## Through the air

The Moon's light crosses the same air as the Sun's, and the same two things happen to it: the air takes some of it
away, more of the blue, and adds its own light in front. nachtgestirn takes the first as a
<Term of="Transmittance">transmittance</Term> per channel along the line of sight, so the rising Moon comes out dim and
orange. Near the horizon a ray half a degree lower crosses far more air: at moonrise the lower limb lets a third as much
green through as the upper, and none of the blue. So the table that bends the rays, below, holds what the air lets
through at each of its altitudes too, from dunstkreis' `airTransmittance`, traced along the same bent path as its
refraction. The second it leaves to the background,
the sky drawn behind it in cd/m², whose light it adds on top of the disc. That is the whole secret of the Moon by day:
it is as bright as at night, the sky in front of it is simply brighter still, and a renderer that draws the Moon over
the daytime sky in physical units gets the pale daytime Moon for free.

The air also bends the Moon's light, and more the lower it comes in: on the horizon it lifts the lower limb some 6′
more than the upper, and the rising Moon shows squeezed to four fifths of its height. The pass bends every ray as
dunstkreis bends them for the sun disc: `refractionTable` asks any refraction you hand it, the figure dunstkreis'
own, how far the air bends a ray at 32 apparent altitudes over where the disc shows, and the pass turns each ray down
by it before it meets the sphere. Seen from orbit, where a ray grazing the planet crosses the air twice and dips deep
into it, the same table squeezes the setting Moon to a quarter of its height, as astronauts photograph it.

The Moon lights the air as the Sun does, at a four hundred thousandth of the light: some 0.3 lux above the air at full
Moon, which `moonIlluminance` gives. The sky by moonlight is the day's sky at that share, blue, with the aureole of the
haze around the disc, and dunstkreis draws it from a second sky-view table built from the Moon's direction when its
sky pass is made with `moonlight`. In <Fig id="moon" />, turn the exposure off auto, take it down to −3 and zoom out:
the night around the full Moon is blue.

That is the camera's blue, the day's sky a million times dimmer. The eye sees the night differently: below some
4 cd/m² its <Term of="Scotopic vision">rods</Term> take over from the cones, which see color, and by moonlight the
rods see alone. Colors fade, red goes dark first, and what is left looks blue, which painters have always given the
night. The pass' `nightVision`, helldunkel's blue shift after Jensen et al. <Cite id="jensen2001" />, turns the composed light into that before
it is exposed: switch the figure to `eye`. The disc itself, at 2,500 cd/m² far brighter than what the rods handle,
keeps its color.

The exposure is the renderer's: the pass takes an EV100 like dunstkreis' sky pass, and the two agree, since both
start from the same 128,000 lux. A full Moon exposes around 14 like a sunny day, the photographer's "looney 11" of
f/11 at the film's speed, which is why a night sky metered for the stars burns the Moon out to a white disc. The figure
meters on the Moon, or by day on the sky, whichever is brighter.

## The people behind the numbers

Bruce Hapke worked out the photometric function of a powdered surface in 1963 and improved its scattering term in 1966
<Cite id="hapke1963" /> <Cite id="hapke1966" />; the full Moon's flatness and the opposition surge are his. Henrik Wann
Jensen and his colleagues put that law, with earthshine and the night sky's other lights, into a renderer in 2001
<Cite id="jensen2001" />, the paper osgHimmel's Moon followed. Hendrik van de Hulst's phase function of a scattering
sphere, from his book on multiple scattering of 1980 <Cite id="vandehulst1980" />, is where the earthshine's
dependence on Earth's phase comes from. The maps are Ernie Wright's, of NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio, who
assembled the CGI Moon Kit in 2019 and remade its color map in December 2025 <Cite id="svs4720" />.
