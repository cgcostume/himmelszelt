This chapter is being written. Its figures still show only the computed sky of dunstkreis.

Not every sky has to be computed. A photographed or pre-rendered one arrives as an image, and this library is about
turning such an image into something a renderer can sample and light with: cube maps, spherical, paraboloid, and
polar projections, converted between each other, in HDR where the source has the range for it. From the same image it
derives what image-based lighting needs, the diffuse irradiance and the pre-filtered specular levels, so a scene lit by
the sky and a scene showing the sky agree with each other.

It also carries the horizon band from the original thesis. Several projections cover only the upper hemisphere and end
in a hard edge at the horizon, which a band of ground color, faded into the lowest rows of the map, hides. The
computed skies of [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) and [sternenheer](../sternenheer/) can be baked into the same maps, so a
renderer can trade a live sky for a captured one without changing anything else. The original's texture mapped skies are
shown in [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y8UMaM_Wj-o).

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, WebGPU compute passes, no dependency on any other package. It takes any cube map as
a plain `GPUTexture`, and exposure and tone curves are [helldunkel](../helldunkel/)'s.

## Lighting a scene

A cube map's faces are six perspective views, and a perspective crowds the most directions into its middle: a texel at a
face's corner covers only a fifth of the <Term of="Solid angle">solid angle</Term> of one at its center. So the sky is resolved five times as finely
towards the corners as in the middle of a face, and where two faces meet, the texel grid turns abruptly, which can show
in gradients as smooth as the sky's. Cubified, as osgHimmel's `CubeMappedHimmel` did it <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />,
the texels are placed by Nowell's mapping of a cube onto a sphere <Cite id="nowell2005" /> instead. The largest then
covers a third more solid angle than the smallest, a little more even than the equi-angular cube map YouTube streams
video in, at 41% more <Cite id="brown2017" />. It costs a few instructions per lookup, and whoever samples the cube map
has to know: it looks up the point on the cube that maps to its direction, not the direction itself. Filtering across
the faces' edges and the mip levels work as before. The two figures below switch it together.

The cube map comes with its mip levels, each texel the mean of four in the level above, down to a single texel a face.
They cost a third more memory and a few cheap passes, and they are optional: the cube pass fills as many levels as the
texture was created with, so a `mipLevelCount` of 1 leaves them out. Two readers need them here. The projection onto the
coefficients takes 4,096 directions; from a face of 1,024 texels it would see only one texel in some 1,500 and miss
whatever lies between, so it reads the level whose texels are about as many as its directions. And a background showing
the cube map through a wide lens spans several texels with each pixel, so it reads the level where a texel is about a
pixel wide, instead of picking one texel of many and flickering as the view turns.

Nine numbers per color channel hold the sky's diffuse light, as a sound is held by a few of its lowest tones (<Fig id="harmonics" />): the first is the
sky's average brightness, the next three say how much brighter it is above than below and towards the Sun than away
from it, and the last five bend that into the shape of a lit horizon over a dark ground. Like overtones over a
fundamental, each band turns faster around the sphere than the one before. A diffuse surface sees the sky blurred over
the whole hemisphere it faces, and nine terms of that blur keep more than 99% of the light it receives, for any sky
<Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />: of band 3 it receives nothing at all. A shader turns them into the light on any surface with nine multiplies and
no texture lookup, which is what `createIrradiancePass` writes into a buffer, next to an irradiance cube map computed
from the same nine for shaders that prefer a lookup.

An image has no depth: all of it lies at infinity, its ground as well. So a solid put into it floats, and the ground slides
along with the camera. Laying everything below the horizon on a plane fixes both for the cost of one intersection per
pixel: the camera's ray meets the plane, and the image is looked up from where it was taken, some way above that plane,
towards that point (<Fig id="ground-geometry" />). Towards the horizon, though, the plane reaches out without end, and a
mountain far off ends up smeared across the ground. [three.js](https://threejs.org/examples/webgl_materials_envmaps_groundprojected.html) and
Unreal's [HDRI Backdrop](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/hdri-backdrop-visualization-tool-in-unreal-engine)
close it off instead: the ground ends in a disc, and the sky lies on a sphere about where the image was taken. The
mountain then stands on the sphere, at the horizon.

A sphere has one radius for all of it, though. Small enough to end the ground before it smears, it brings the sky in as
well, and the mountain slides past the solids as the camera moves: on a dome of 30 m, a step of 6 m to the side shifts
it by 11°, some twenty times the Moon's width. rundbild keeps the two apart (<Fig id="grounded" />). The ground lies
flat as far as its reach, the horizon and the sky lie on a dome with a radius of its own, and in between the ground
bends up from the one into the other, leaving the plane level and meeting the dome at its slope, never under the plane.
How high up the dome it meets it is a choice of its own: at most where the image's horizon does, as high as the camera
for a level horizon, at least where the plane meets the dome, a hard corner. At the longest reach for that height the
bend is a circle, and closer in a flatter arc (<Fig id="ground-geometry" />). The dome can be without end, where a sky
belongs: the ground then falls away past its reach like a hilltop, and the mountain stays at infinity.
Within the reach, `rbGroundedDirection` still costs the one intersection, and past it up to fifteen steps along the ray,
which close in on the surface.

Far out on the ground, a few rows of the image cover a great deal of it. With the photograph taken 2 m up, the degree of
it that ends 30 m out, about eleven rows of a 4K image, spans more than 6 m, while across it every row stays as sharp as
before. Looked up as they are, those rows draw out into streaks that run away from where the photograph was taken. The
usual cure, a <Term of="Mipmap">mipmap</Term>, blurs both ways at once and keeps the streaks, only softer. What helps is
to blur along the azimuth alone, as far as the stretch leaves the image short along its elevation. `rbStretchedLods`
measures the stretch from where a pixel and its two neighbors look up and returns how far to blur along each axis, and
levels of the image halved more often along the azimuth than along the elevation hold that blur ready, up to 64 to 1.

What remains are the photograph's own features: no filter adds rows the camera never took, and what truly rises from the
ground within its reach, a tree or a house, lies flat and smears the further the camera moves from where the photograph
was taken. So the reach is best no longer than the ground is flat, and the camera best within it. Lifting the horizon is
simpler still: the image is stretched between its poles, for a horizon that is not where the image has it. All of these
are `wgsl.ground`, functions from a direction to a direction, for any map, and the plane without end is still there,
with the reach and the dome at their far ends.

## Flattening the sky

A texture is flat and the sky is not. Storing one in the other is the mapmaker's old problem, and every answer to it is
a map projection that pays somewhere: where texels crowd, they are wasted, and where they thin out, the sky blurs. The
cube map pays at its faces' corners. The panorama HDRIs are shared in, on sites like
[Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com/hdris) <Cite id="polyhaven" />, is the
<Term of="Equirectangular projection">equirectangular projection</Term>, and it is nearly two thousand years old:
Marinus of Tyre is credited with it, around 100 CE <Cite id="snyder1987" />. The azimuth runs across it and the zenith
angle down, so a single direction, the zenith, is stretched over the whole top row.

An HDRI has no north, only wherever its camera happened to point. rundbild sets one: north at the left edge, east a
quarter in, south in the middle and west three quarters in, as seen from inside, so the Sun's arc over a northern sky
stays clear of the seam. `azimuthOffset` turns it about the zenith, 180 for a southern sky with north in the middle. At
about 400 texels, the equirectangular's largest texel covers some nine times the solid angle of its smallest.

The <Term of="Octahedral map">octahedral map</Term> folds the sphere onto an octahedron and unfolds that into a square
<Cite id="engelhardt2008" />: the zenith in the middle, the upper hemisphere a diamond without a seam, and the texels
within a factor of about five. The fisheye stores what an all-sky camera sees, the upper hemisphere in a circle, its
distance from the middle in proportion to the zenith angle. It spreads its texels within a factor of about 1.6, but
leaves the corners of its square to no direction, a fifth of it. rundbild fills them with the horizon at their
azimuth, so a filtered lookup at the rim blends into the sky rather than into black. Only the top left corner texel
holds the ground's color, as a cube map holds it straight down, and every direction below the horizon looks it up
there. The sphere map is the mirror ball's: the whole sphere in the circle, the zenith in the middle and the nadir all
round the rim, the distance from the middle the sine of half the zenith angle. That keeps every texel's solid angle
the same, and the horizon at seven tenths of the radius. Octahedral, fisheye and sphere map are laid out as a sky chart
held overhead: north up and east left, since the sky is seen from below.

The cube map lights a scene best, filtered and mipmapped by the hardware; the equirectangular goes to every other tool;
the octahedral packs into a 2D atlas of many, as light probes are; the fisheye compares with photographs of the whole
sky, and the sphere map holds all of it in one disc. `createPanoramaPass` lays any cube map out as one of the four (<Fig id="projections" />).
It spreads `samples` lookups over every texel, which smooth the horizon and the fisheye's rim within it.

## From one to the other

A map turned into another is resampled, and resampling loses what falls between texels unless the target is the finer of
the two. An equirectangular of W texels across spends 2π/W radians on a texel at the horizon, a cube map of N texels a
face 2/N at the middle of a face: N has to reach W/π, about a third of the width, not the quarter the faces' count
suggests. A turn into a cube map and back then keeps the sky, up to the filter's softness. The octahedral map needs care
of its own: its lower half folds outward, so a filtered lookup across its edge reads a neighbor that is not one on the
sphere. `createCubeFromPanoramaPass` and `createPanoramaPass` go each way. Read with `decodeRgbe`, an HDRI goes into a
cube map as sharp as itself and out again as an octahedral map, on your `device`:

```ts
import {
    createCubeFromPanoramaPass,
    createPanoramaPass,
    decodeRgbe,
} from "@himmelszelt/rundbild";

const file = await (await fetch("sky.hdr")).arrayBuffer();
const { width, height, pixels } = decodeRgbe(file);
// The passes read half floats.
const panorama = device.createTexture({
    size: [width, height],
    format: "rgba16float",
    usage: GPUTextureUsage.TEXTURE_BINDING | GPUTextureUsage.COPY_DST,
});
device.queue.writeTexture(
    { texture: panorama },
    new Float16Array(pixels),
    { bytesPerRow: width * 8 },
    [width, height],
);

// As sharp at the horizon: a face a third of the width, 1304 for 4K.
const face = Math.ceil(width / Math.PI);
const cube = device.createTexture({
    size: [face, face, 6],
    mipLevelCount: Math.floor(Math.log2(face)) + 1,
    format: "rgba16float",
    usage: GPUTextureUsage.STORAGE_BINDING | GPUTextureUsage.TEXTURE_BINDING,
    textureBindingViewDimension: "cube",
});
// Octahedral, as sharp: 0.73 of the width, 2973 for 4K.
const side = Math.ceil((width * Math.sqrt(3 * Math.sqrt(3))) / Math.PI);
const octahedral = device.createTexture({
    size: [side, side],
    format: "rgba16float",
    usage: GPUTextureUsage.STORAGE_BINDING | GPUTextureUsage.COPY_SRC,
});

const toCube = await createCubeFromPanoramaPass(device);
const toOctahedral = await createPanoramaPass(device, {
    projection: "octahedral", // or "equirectangular", "fisheye", "sphere"
    format: "rgba16float",
    samples: 4,
});
const encoder = device.createCommandEncoder();
toCube.encode(encoder, panorama, cube);
toOctahedral.encode(encoder, cube, octahedral);
device.queue.submit([encoder.finish()]);
```

Sized that way, by the largest texel each may have, the projections hold the same sky in very different amounts of
memory (<Fig id="storage" />). The cube map, which the hardware filters best, holds a fifth more texels than the
equirectangular it came from: a 4K HDRI's 64 MiB in half floats become 78 MiB in faces of 1,304, as its fine corners
waste more than the equirectangular's poles do. Cubified, the same sky fits in faces of 991 and 45 MiB, three tenths
less, its texels within a third of each other. The octahedral map costs a twentieth more than the equirectangular: it
spreads its texels about as unevenly as a cube map, but in one square without a seam. The sphere map, a fifth less,
gives every texel the same share of the sky, yet leaves a fifth of its square to no direction and squeezes the nadir
into its rim, where a texel is a sliver: even in area is not even in shape.

A sky above a ground drawn by other means needs only its upper half. The equirectangular then ends at the horizon, four
times as wide as high, and the cube map becomes a hemicube, its top face and the upper halves of its four sides, as
Michael Cohen and Donald Greenberg laid out what a surface sees for radiosity <Cite id="cohen1985" />. Either holds half
of what its whole sphere takes, and the fisheye was a hemisphere's from the start.

How a texel is stored counts as much. Half floats take 8 bytes, rg11b10ufloat and rgb9e5ufloat 4, and
<Term of="Block compression">BC6H</Term>, encoded ahead of time, a single byte <Cite id="microsoft2018bc6h" />.
rundbild's passes write half or full floats; the others are for keeping a map once it is made. Light probes, packed
by the thousand into one atlas, take a gutter of a texel around each, a copy of its edge for the filter
<Cite id="majercik2019" />, and mip levels only as far down as the gutters hold.

Mip levels pay the same way. Each texel of a level is the mean of four above it, which treats them alike: nearly right on
a cubified cube map, a little off towards a plain one's corners, and far off on an equirectangular, whose top rows are
one direction many times over. So the mip levels live on the cube map. A cubified map keeps its evenness only for whoever
knows: another tool reads it as a plain one, bent, so a file for others goes through `createPanoramaPass` first.

Whoever samples an equirectangular directly, in a shader of their own, meets its two flaws. At a pole, one direction
stretches over a whole row: at 4K, a texel of the top row holds some 1,300 times less of the sky than one at the
horizon, and a filter there blends neighbors that lie a hair apart. Summing the image, for the sky's mean light or to
pick its bright texels more often by <Term of="Importance sampling">importance sampling</Term>, has to weigh every row
by the sine of its zenith angle, the solid angle its texels hold, and a direction picked by its texel has its density
divided by that sine, which grows without end towards the poles <Cite id="pharr2016" />.

The seam is the subtler flaw. A fragment shader's `textureSample` takes its mip level from how far the coordinates move
between neighboring pixels <Cite id="w3c2026wgsl" />, and across the seam u jumps from 1 to 0: the whole image within
one pixel, so it reads the coarsest level, the sky's mean color, in a line one pixel wide down the sky. The cure takes
the step by hand, where a jump of a whole turn is no step at all, through a sampler that repeats along u:

```wgsl
// d is the unit direction a pixel looks at; wgsl.panorama goes in front.
fn equirectangular(sky: texture_2d<f32>, s: sampler, d: vec3f) -> vec4f {
    let uv = rbEquirectangularUv(d, 0.0);
    // Across the seam u jumps by one, a whole turn, which is no turn at all.
    var dx = dpdx(uv);
    var dy = dpdy(uv);
    dx.x -= round(dx.x);
    dy.x -= round(dy.x);
    return textureSampleGrad(sky, s, uv, dx, dy);
}
```

A cube map has neither flaw, the other reason the mip levels live there. rundbild itself reads an equirectangular only
in compute passes, at a level it names, wraps the azimuth the same way in `rbStretchedLods`, and takes the nine
coefficients from directions spread evenly over the sphere, which need no weights.

Two environments fade by their light, not by their pictures, so a mix of a noon and a night stays physically a mix of
the two. `createEnvironmentBlendPass` writes it into a cube map, and the nine coefficients of the result are those of the
two mixed by the same share, `mixCoefficients`, without a pass at all. A sky in cd/m² and an HDRI of relative values take
a scale each first.

## What it costs

An HDRI is dear to hold and cheap to use. The chapter's first, Poly Haven's file of 4K, downloads 24 MiB once and stays
in the browser; on the GPU its pixels in half floats take 64 MiB, and a cube map as sharp as the panorama, [a third of
its width](#from-one-to-the-other) rounded up to a power of two, 2048 a face, another 256 MiB, made in a millisecond.
Lighting asks for far less: a cube map of 256 a face, four lookups a texel, takes a twelfth of a millisecond and 4 MiB,
and the panorama may go once it is made. Everything after that costs less than a frame of a computed sky: the nine
coefficients and the irradiance 0.03 ms, a fade of two environments 512 a face 0.06 ms, a panorama laid out 0.03 ms.
The package downloads some 14 KiB of code, gzipped (<Fig id="costs" />). The times are taken as [dunstkreis'
are](../dunstkreis/#what-it-costs).

## The people behind the numbers

Ravi Ramamoorthi and Pat Hanrahan, at Stanford, showed in 2001 that nine numbers hold the diffuse light of any
environment <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />.

An image stands still, with its Sun where the camera found it. For a sky that moves, [sternzeit](../sternzeit/) says where
the Sun is, and [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) computes what the air makes of its light, as live as the baked sky above.
