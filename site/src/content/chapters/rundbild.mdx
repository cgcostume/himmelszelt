This chapter is being written. Its figures still show only the computed sky of dunstkreis; HDRIs follow with the loader.

Not every sky has to be computed. A photographed or pre-rendered one arrives as an image, and this library is about
turning such an image into something a renderer can sample and light with: cube maps, spherical, paraboloid, and
polar projections, converted between each other, in HDR where the source has the range for it. From the same image it
derives what image-based lighting needs, the diffuse irradiance and the pre-filtered specular levels, so a scene lit by
the sky and a scene showing the sky agree with each other.

It also carries the horizon band from the original thesis. Several projections cover only the upper hemisphere and end
in a hard edge at the horizon, which a band of ground color, faded into the lowest rows of the map, hides. The
computed skies of [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) and [sternenheer](../sternenheer/) can be baked into the same maps, so a
renderer can trade a live sky for a captured one without changing anything else. The original's texture mapped skies are
shown in [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y8UMaM_Wj-o).

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, WebGPU compute passes, no dependency on any other package. It takes any cube map as
a plain `GPUTexture`, and exposure and tone curves are [helldunkel](../helldunkel/)'s.

## Lighting a scene

A cube map's faces are six perspective views, and a perspective crowds the most directions into its middle: a texel at a
face's corner covers only a fifth of the solid angle of one at its center. So the sky is resolved five times as finely
towards the corners as in the middle of a face, and where two faces meet, the texel grid turns abruptly, which can show
in gradients as smooth as the sky's. Cubified, as osgHimmel's `CubeMappedHimmel` did it <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />,
the texels are placed by Nowell's mapping of a cube onto a sphere <Cite id="nowell2005" /> instead. The largest then
covers a third more solid angle than the smallest, a little more even than the equi-angular cube map YouTube streams
video in, at 41% more <Cite id="brown2017" />. It costs a few instructions per lookup, and whoever samples the cube map
has to know: it looks up the point on the cube that maps to its direction, not the direction itself. Filtering across
the faces' edges and the mip levels work as before. The two figures below switch it together.

The cube map comes with its mip levels, each texel the mean of four in the level above, down to a single texel a face.
They cost a third more memory and a few cheap passes, and they are optional: the cube pass fills as many levels as the
texture was created with, so a `mipLevelCount` of 1 leaves them out. Two readers need them here. The projection onto the
coefficients takes 4,096 directions; from a face of 1,024 texels it would see only one texel in some 1,500 and miss
whatever lies between, so it reads the level whose texels are about as many as its directions. And a background showing
the cube map through a wide lens spans several texels with each pixel, so it reads the level where a texel is about a
pixel wide, instead of picking one texel of many and flickering as the view turns.

Nine numbers per color channel hold the sky's diffuse light, as a sound is held by a few of its lowest tones (<Fig id="harmonics" />): the first is the
sky's average brightness, the next three say how much brighter it is above than below and towards the Sun than away
from it, and the last five bend that into the shape of a lit horizon over a dark ground. A diffuse surface sees the
sky blurred over the whole hemisphere it faces, and nine terms of that blur keep more than 99% of the light it receives,
for any sky <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />. A shader turns them into the light on any surface with nine multiplies and
no texture lookup, which is what `createIrradiancePass` writes into a buffer, next to an irradiance cube map computed
from the same nine for shaders that prefer a lookup.

## Flattening the sky

A texture is flat and the sky is not. Storing one in the other is the mapmaker's old problem, and every answer to it is
a map projection that pays somewhere: where texels crowd, they are wasted, and where they thin out, the sky blurs. The
cube map pays at its faces' corners. The panorama HDRIs are shared in, on sites like
[Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com/hdris) <Cite id="polyhaven" />, is the
<Term of="Equirectangular projection">equirectangular projection</Term>, and it is nearly two thousand years old:
Marinus of Tyre is credited with it, around 100 CE <Cite id="snyder1987" />. The azimuth runs across it and the zenith
angle down, so a single direction, the zenith, is stretched over the whole top row.

An HDRI has no north, only wherever its camera happened to point. rundbild sets one: north at the left edge, east a
quarter in, south in the middle and west three quarters in, as seen from inside, so the Sun's arc over a northern sky
stays clear of the seam. `azimuthOffset` turns it about the zenith, 180 for a southern sky with north in the middle. At
about 400 texels, the equirectangular's largest texel covers some nine times the solid angle of its smallest.

The <Term of="Octahedral map">octahedral map</Term> folds the sphere onto an octahedron and unfolds that into a square
<Cite id="engelhardt2008" />: the zenith in the middle, the upper hemisphere a diamond without a seam, and the texels
within a factor of about five. The fisheye stores what an all-sky camera sees, the upper hemisphere in a circle, its
distance from the middle in proportion to the zenith angle. It spreads its texels within a factor of about 1.6, but
leaves the corners of its square to no direction, a fifth of it. rundbild fills them with the horizon at their
azimuth, so a filtered lookup at the rim blends into the sky rather than into black. Only the top left corner texel
holds the ground's color, as a cube map holds it straight down, and every direction below the horizon looks it up
there. The sphere map is the mirror ball's: the whole sphere in the circle, the zenith in the middle and the nadir all
round the rim, the distance from the middle the sine of half the zenith angle. That keeps every texel's solid angle
the same, and the horizon at seven tenths of the radius. Octahedral, fisheye and sphere map are laid out as a sky chart
held overhead: north up and east left, since the sky is seen from below.

The cube map lights a scene best, filtered and mipmapped by the hardware; the equirectangular goes to every other tool;
the octahedral packs into a 2D atlas of many, as light probes are; the fisheye compares with photographs of the whole
sky, and the sphere map holds all of it in one disc. `createPanoramaPass` lays any cube map out as one of the four (<Fig id="projections" />).
It spreads `samples` lookups over every texel, which smooth the horizon and the fisheye's rim within it.

## The people behind the numbers

Ravi Ramamoorthi and Pat Hanrahan, at Stanford, showed in 2001 that nine numbers hold the diffuse light of any
environment <Cite id="ramamoorthi2001" />.

An image stands still, with its Sun where the camera found it. For a sky that moves, [sternzeit](../sternzeit/) says where
the Sun is, and [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) computes what the air makes of its light, as live as the baked sky above.
