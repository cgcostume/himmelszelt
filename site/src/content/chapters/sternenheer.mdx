This chapter is being written. The sky below is live, and it grows with the chapter.

Look up on a clear night far from town and you see some two thousand stars, and on the best nights a band of faint
light across them. Each star is a point, too far away to have any size at all, so a renderer cannot ask how large it
is, only how much light it sends: a star of <Term of="Apparent magnitude">magnitude</Term> 0 lights your eye with
some 2.6 millionths of a lux, the faintest you can see a four hundredth of that <Cite id="cox2000" />. sternenheer
draws the stars from that number: every star of the Yale Bright Star Catalogue <Cite id="hoffleit1991" />, nine
thousand of them, all the sky holds to the naked eye, at its light in lux, spread over the image the eye or a camera
makes of a point, with the Milky Way behind them from NASA's map of the stars too faint for the catalog
<Cite id="svs4851" />, calibrated to the measured light of all the stars together <Cite id="masana2021" />. Where
the stars stand tonight comes from [sternzeit](../sternzeit/) as one rotation; the air that lifts them near the
horizon, dims them and makes them twinkle, and the sky they shine through, from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/); the
Moon over them from [nachtgestirn](../nachtgestirn/). osgHimmel drew the same catalog as points of a size set by hand
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />; here the light is in lux and the pixels in cd/m², so the stars expose like the sky and
the Moon, and come and go with the exposure as they do for the eye.

For a developer: TypeScript and WGSL, two WebGPU compute passes, no dependency on any other package at runtime. One
pass bins every star's image into the tiles of the target it reaches, the other sums the stars of each pixel's tile
and the Milky Way behind, over a background in cd/m², and tone maps or stays linear, as dunstkreis' sky does. What it
costs is on the line under <Fig id="stars" />: a fraction of a millisecond.

The sky below is those passes over dunstkreis' sky, under nachtgestirn's Moon, a wide view towards the south: drag to
look around, scroll to zoom in on a star, and set the moment and the place under it, which every figure of the chapter
follows.

Four lines are the whole setup: the catalog and the map once, the pass once, and per frame the rotation sternzeit
computes for the moment and the place, `stars.horizontalMatrix`, with the camera, the exposure and the air.

```ts
import { airTable, createStarsPass, loadCatalog, loadMilkyWay } from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";

const catalog = await loadCatalog();
const milkyWay = await loadMilkyWay(device);
const pass = await createStarsPass(device, { format: "rgba8unorm", catalog, milkyWay });
// Per frame: the sky's rotation, the camera, the exposure, and the air.
pass.update({ skyRotation, inverseViewProjection, ev100: -9, air: airTable(bend, through) });
pass.encode(encoder, target, { background: skyInCandelaPerSquareMeter });
```

## Nine thousand stars

Hipparchus sorted the stars he could see into six classes of brightness, the first for the brightest, the sixth for
the faintest, and the scale never left: a star of the sixth magnitude is a hundred times fainter than one of the
first, each magnitude a factor of 2.512, and the brightest stars run past zero into negative numbers, Sirius at
−1.46, the Sun at −26.74 <Cite id="cox2000" />. A renderer wants light, not classes, so sternenheer turns the scale
around: `illuminanceFromMagnitude` gives the illuminance at the eye in lux, pinned to the Sun's 128,000 lux that
dunstkreis starts from, which puts a magnitude 0 star at 2.6 millionths of a lux, Sirius at ten millionths, and a star
of 6.5, the naked eye's limit under a dark sky, at six billionths.

The catalog is the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, fifth edition, 9,093 stars to magnitude 7.1 <Cite id="hoffleit1991" />,
8,403 of them within the naked eye's reach, shipped as 291 KB of eight numbers per star: the magnitude, the position
at J2000.0, the star's own motion, and its color. All of them together give a magnitude of −5.0, about as much light
as Venus at its brightest; the stars you see are a dusting, and most of the night's starlight comes from the ones you
cannot, which is where [the Milky Way](#the-milky-way) comes in.

## Points of light

A star has no size, but its image has: your eye blurs a point into a spot about an arcminute across, on the sky and
on a screen alike. sternenheer spreads each star's light over a Gaussian
<Term of="Point spread function">point spread function</Term> of a width in pixels, under one by default, the viewer's
own blur, and never narrower than a pixel, so no star falls between two. What the pixel then holds is a luminance in
cd/m², the star's lux over the spot's solid angle on the sky, and that solid angle shrinks as the view zooms in: a
star does not grow with the magnification, it brightens, as it does in a telescope, while the Milky Way and the sky,
spread over area, stay as they are. In a view of the whole sky Sirius peaks at a fraction of a cd/m², a thousand times
the night sky, and burns out to a white point at any exposure that shows the Milky Way, while a star of the sixth
magnitude stays a dim one. No magnitude limit decides which stars show; the exposure does, as it does for the eye, and
a star too faint for it is simply not there. Set the exposure of <Fig id="stars" /> by hand and open it stop by stop:
the stars come up in order of their magnitudes, the faint ones last.

The eye adds a veil. A bright point scatters a little of its light across the whole retina, the same
<Term of="Veiling glare">veiling glare</Term> the Sun throws in dunstkreis, which CIE's equation gives as a luminance
per lux by the angle to the source <Cite id="cie2002" />: around Sirius it is a halo a degree wide on your own field of
view, which the pass takes as the angle the picture spans at your eye, around the fainter stars nothing the sky does not
outshine. Switch the figure's `glare` off, and every bright star shrinks to its spot.

## The sky turning

A catalog gives a star's place for one moment, the <Term of="Standard equinox">standard equinox</Term> of
2000, in <Term of="Equatorial coordinates">equatorial coordinates</Term>, and the sky
has moved since. Earth turns under the stars once a sidereal day, which is sternzeit's
<Term of="Sidereal time">sidereal time</Term>, and its axis slowly turns too, the
<Term of="Precession">precession</Term> of the equinoxes, some 50 arcseconds a year along the ecliptic
<Cite id="meeus1998" />: since 2000 Vega has drifted 11 arcminutes from its catalog place, a third of the Moon's
width, more than a pixel of any view of the whole sky. The stars' own motion, their
<Term of="Proper motion">proper motion</Term>, is far smaller: the fastest star in the catalog, Groombridge 1830,
crosses a Moon's width in 260 years.

sternzeit does the astronomy, as it does for the Sun and the Moon: `stars.apparentPosition` moves one star by its
proper motion and precesses it to the date, per Meeus' chapter 21, and `stars.horizontalMatrix` folds the precession and
the sidereal time at your place into one rotation from the catalog's frame into your east, north and up. The pass takes
that rotation and turns nine thousand stars by it on the GPU, each one moved by its own proper motion first. Step the
moment a day at a time in <Fig id="stars" />, and the whole sky turns a degree, four minutes earlier each night. A y-up
engine takes the directions as `(x, z, -y)`.

```ts
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, stars } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const time = fromDate(new Date());
const observer = { latitude, longitude };
// One rotation for the whole catalog: precession and sidereal time in one.
const skyRotation = stars.horizontalMatrix(time, observer);
// The stars' own motion is per star: the years since J2000.0.
const years = (julianEphemerisDay(time) - 2451545) / 365.25;
```

## The colors of the stars

Stars have colors, faint to the eye and plain to a camera: Betelgeuse is orange, Vega blue-white, and the catalog
carries the difference as a <Term of="Color index">color index</Term>, B minus V, how much brighter a star is through
a blue filter than through a yellow one. A star radiates close to a black body, so the index gives a temperature,
by Ballesteros' formula <Cite id="ballesteros2012" />, 10,100 K for Vega, 5,800 K for the Sun, 3,300 K for Betelgeuse,
and a temperature gives a chromaticity on the Planckian locus <Cite id="kim2002" /> and from there a color in linear
sRGB at unit luminance, which the magnitude then scales. `colorFromColorIndex` does the three steps; the shipped
catalog holds the result of the approximate path osgHimmel took, after Jensen et al. <Cite id="jensen2001" />, which
runs hot, 12,500 K for Vega, and makes the blue stars a little bluer. The colors are saturated on purpose: a star's
light is a black body's, and the eye at night, which sees little color, is [the night vision](#through-the-air)
switch's business.

## Through the air

The air does three things to a star's light, all of them worst near the horizon. It bends the light, so every star
shows higher than it is: on the horizon by some 35 arcminutes, more than the Moon's width, and a star that is truly
on the horizon already stands half a degree up. The pass takes the air as a table over the apparent altitude,
`airTable`, filled from any refraction you hand it, the figure dunstkreis' own, traced through the same air the sky
is drawn from, and its entries crowd towards the horizon, where the bend changes fastest. It lifts every star by it,
looks the Milky Way up along the unbent ray, and draws nothing below the lowest ray that still reaches space, which
from the ground lies a little below the geometric horizon and from orbit far below it.

It takes light away, more of the blue: at the zenith the air lets 87% of the green through and 77% of the blue, at
5° up a quarter of the green and a sixteenth of the blue, on the horizon under one percent of anything, which is why
a rising star is red and faint and why the Milky Way fades into the haze long before the horizon. The same table holds
dunstkreis' `airTransmittance` at every altitude, and the pass dims each star and each pixel of the Milky Way by it.

And it twinkles. The air's turbulence bends a star's light this way and that on its way down, and a pupil seven
millimeters wide catches now more of it, now less: the light's logarithm scatters by a fifth near the zenith and
grows with the airmass to the power 1.75, Young's scaling as Dravins and colleagues give it for a small aperture and a
short integration <Cite id="dravins1998" />, doubling by 30° up and saturating there. Near the horizon the air also
disperses the star's light into a tiny spectrum, and the red and the blue twinkle apart, which is the flashing of
colors a low Sirius is famous for. The pass draws the star's light with a random factor per channel, smooth in time
and correlated across the channels high up, independent low down, from a hash of the star and the time: switch the
figure's `twinkle` on and watch the horizon. A planet, a disc rather than a point, twinkles far less, and sternenheer
has none yet.

The sky behind the stars is dunstkreis': by day it outshines all of them, by moonlight the fainter ones, and the
figure meters on it where it is brighter than the stars' own light. The eye sees the night differently than a camera:
below some 4 cd/m² its rods take over, colors fade and shift towards blue, and helldunkel's blue shift turns the
composed light into that before it is exposed, the figure's `night vision` switch, as in nachtgestirn.

## The Milky Way

Most of the night's starlight comes from stars too faint to see one by one, and together they are the band across
the sky. sternenheer draws them from NASA's Deep Star Maps of 2020 <Cite id="svs4851" />, the map of the Milky Way
without the stars of Hipparcos and Tycho, which holds the light of the stars of Gaia's catalog fainter than the
eleventh magnitude, 1.7 billion of them, in the same J2000 equatorial coordinates as the catalog, so the one rotation
turns both. osgHimmel drew a photograph of the Milky Way as a cube map with an intensity set by hand
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />; the map is calibrated instead. Masana and colleagues measured the light of all the
stars together, the <Term of="Integrated starlight">integrated starlight</Term>, from the same Gaia data: at the zenith
of a place at 40° north it averages a <Term of="Surface brightness">surface brightness</Term> of 23.2 magnitudes per
square arcsecond over a year, a fifth of the natural night sky's light <Cite id="masana2021" />. The map plus the
catalog are scaled to give that along the band of the sky that zenith sweeps, which puts the galactic center at 21.0
magnitudes per square arcsecond, brighter than a dark sky's 21.5, the galactic poles at 25.5, far below it. The band
you see in the figure is the band you see on a dark night, at the light the sky gives it: zoom out and dim the
exposure, and it fades into the sky before the stars do.

## Precise and approximate

Two pieces come in a precise and a cheaper variant. The precession is Meeus' three angles and their rotation, or
Jensen et al.'s linear approximation of them <Cite id="jensen2001" />, which sternzeit offers as `stars` under its
`approx` entry: within an arcsecond of each other over this century, far below a pixel, so the approximate one is
the one to take. The star's temperature is Ballesteros' formula or Jensen's, and here the difference shows: Jensen's
makes Vega 12,500 K instead of 10,100 and Betelgeuse 2,900 K instead of 3,300, which is bluer blue stars and redder
red ones, the shipped catalog's colors. `colorFromColorIndex` and `colorFromColorIndexApprox` are both there, to recolor
a catalog either way.

## The people behind the numbers

Dorrit Hoffleit kept the Yale Bright Star Catalogue through its editions for half a century, to the fifth of 1991
with Wayne Warren that the stars here come from <Cite id="hoffleit1991" />. Jean Meeus' chapter on precession gives
the three angles and the rotation sternzeit turns the catalog by <Cite id="meeus1998" />. Henrik Wann Jensen and his
colleagues put the stars into a renderer in lux in 2001, with the approximate precession and the temperature formula
osgHimmel took from them <Cite id="jensen2001" />. Fernando Ballesteros derived the temperature from the color index
by treating a star as a black body seen through two filters <Cite id="ballesteros2012" />, and Kim and colleagues fit
the Planckian locus that turns the temperature into a color <Cite id="kim2002" />. Andrew Young worked out how the
twinkling scales with the air, which Dainis Dravins and his colleagues measured over three papers in the nineties
<Cite id="dravins1998" />. The Milky Way's map is NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio's <Cite id="svs4851" />, and
its calibration rests on Eduard Masana and colleagues' map of the natural night sky, which summed the light of Gaia's
stars <Cite id="masana2021" />.
