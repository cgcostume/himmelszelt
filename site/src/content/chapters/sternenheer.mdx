This chapter is being written. The sky below is live, and it grows with the chapter.

Look up on a clear night far from town and you see some two thousand stars, and on the best nights a band of faint light
across them. Each star is a point, too far away to have any size at all, so a renderer cannot ask how large it is, only
how much light it sends: a star of <Term of="Apparent magnitude">magnitude</Term> 0 lights your eye with some 2.6
millionths of a lux, the faintest you can see with a four hundredth of that <Cite id="cox2000" />. sternenheer draws the
stars from that number: every star of the Yale Bright Star Catalogue <Cite id="hoffleit1991" />, nine thousand of them,
all the sky holds to the naked eye, each at its light in lux, spread over the image the eye or a camera makes of a
point. Behind them lies the Milky Way, from NASA's map of the stars too faint for the catalog <Cite id="svs4851" />,
calibrated to the measured light of all the stars together <Cite id="masana2021" />. Where the stars stand tonight comes
from [sternzeit](../sternzeit/) as one rotation; the air that lifts them near the horizon, dims them and makes them
twinkle, and the sky they shine through, from [dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/); the Moon over them from
[nachtgestirn](../nachtgestirn/). osgHimmel drew the same catalog as points of a size set by hand
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />; here the light is in lux and the pixels in cd/m², so the stars expose like the sky and
the Moon, and come and go with the exposure as they do for the eye.

`@himmelszelt/sternenheer` is TypeScript and WGSL: a handful of WebGPU compute passes, with no other package needed at
runtime. One pass sorts every star's image into the tiles of the target it reaches, a pyramid of blurs spreads the glow
of the brightest, and the last sums the stars of each pixel's tile and the Milky Way behind them, over a background in
cd/m², and tone maps or stays linear, as dunstkreis' sky does. What it costs is on the line under <Fig id="stars" />: a
fraction of a millisecond. What it is for: a night sky behind any scene, exposed like the rest of it, the stars over a
date and a place of your choosing, or a star chart with [the constellations' lines](#lines-between-the-stars). Two
sections look inside: [how a frame is made](#a-frame-pass-by-pass), and [where the data comes
from](#the-data-and-its-scripts), its scripts included.

The sky below is those passes over dunstkreis' sky, under nachtgestirn's Moon, a wide view towards the south: drag to
look around, scroll to zoom in on a star, and set the moment and the place under it, which every figure of the chapter
follows.

In code, it takes one install, the catalog, the map and the pass once, and a handful of numbers per frame: the rotation

```sh

    @himmelszelt/dunstkreis
```

```ts
import {
    airTable, createAirTexture, DEFAULT_ATMOSPHERE_MODEL,
} from "@himmelszelt/dunstkreis";
import {
    createStarsPass, loadCatalog, loadMilkyWay,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";
import { fromDate, stars } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const catalog = await loadCatalog();
const milkyWay = await loadMilkyWay(device);
const pass = await createStarsPass(device, {
    format: "rgba8unorm", catalog, milkyWay,
});
// The air seen from the ground, made anew only when the height changes.
const table = airTable(DEFAULT_ATMOSPHERE_MODEL, 0);
const air = {
    texture: createAirTexture(device, table), horizon: table.horizon,
};

// Per frame: the sky's rotation for the moment and the place, the camera,
// the exposure, and the air.
const observer = { latitude, longitude };
pass.update({
    skyRotation: stars.horizontalMatrix(fromDate(new Date()), observer),
    inverseViewProjection, // your camera, as dunstkreis' sky pass takes it
    ev100: -9, // a moonless night
    air,
});
pass.encode(encoder, target, { background: sky }); // the sky in cd/m²
```

## Nine thousand stars

Hipparchus sorted the stars he could see into six classes of brightness, the first for the brightest, the sixth for the
faintest, and the scale never left: a star of the sixth magnitude is a hundred times fainter than one of the first, each
magnitude a factor of 2.512, and the brightest stars run past zero into negative numbers, Sirius at −1.46, the Sun at
−26.74 <Cite id="cox2000" />. A renderer wants light, not classes, so sternenheer turns the scale around:
`illuminanceFromMagnitude` gives the illuminance at the eye in lux, pinned to the Sun's 128,000 lux that dunstkreis
starts from, which puts a magnitude 0 star at 2.6 millionths of a lux, Sirius at ten millionths, and a star of 6.5, the
naked eye's limit under a dark sky, at six billionths.

The catalog is the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, fifth edition <Cite id="hoffleit1991" />: 9,093 stars, 8,403 of them
within the naked eye's reach, the rest a little beyond it, down to magnitude 8. A star is one row of six numbers, and
this is Sirius: its magnitude, its <Term of="Equatorial coordinates">right ascension</Term> in hours and its declination
in degrees at <Term of="J2000.0">J2000.0</Term>, how far it moves in each over a year, in
<Term of="Arcsecond">arcseconds</Term>, and its <Term of="Color index">color index</Term>.

```txt
vmag,ra,de,pmRa,pmDe,bv
-1.46,6.752472,-16.71611,-0.553,-1.205,0
```

The rest of this section and the next two take that row to the screen: the magnitude becomes light, [the color index a
color](#the-colors-of-the-stars), and the two together [an image a few pixels wide](#points-of-light). The position
waits for [the sky's turning](#the-sky-turning). All nine thousand together give a magnitude of −5.0, about as much
light as Venus at its brightest; the stars you see are a dusting, and most of the night's starlight comes from the ones
you cannot, which is where [the Milky Way](#the-milky-way) comes in.

Between the two lie the stars a pair of binoculars shows. `loadFaintStars` brings those of the Hipparcos catalogue <Cite
id="esa1997" />, the satellite's 118,218 less those the Yale catalog holds already and a few without a position: 108,883
stars, complete to somewhere between magnitude 7.3 and 9, depending on the part of the sky and the kind of star, with
stragglers down to 12 and beyond. Twelve times as many stars, and together they give a magnitude of −4.4, six tenths of
the light of the nine thousand: faint stars are many, but not many enough. They come packed, 6 bytes a star, in 579 KB
compressed: sorted into cells of the sky, the place in the cell in 16 bits each way, under half an arcsecond, the
magnitude in sixteenths, the color index in hundredths, and no motion of their own. The pass takes them as `faintStars`
next to the catalog and draws the brightest so many of them, which its parameter of the same name sets. ESA's terms keep
them out of the npm package: one command makes them, as [the data's section](#the-data-and-its-scripts) shows. In the
figure they are the middle catalog button. Zoom in, and they come up between the catalog's stars: a star brightens with
the square of the zoom, as [its image](#points-of-light) will explain, so every doubling reaches a magnitude and a half
deeper.

```ts
import {
    createStarsPass, illuminanceFromMagnitude, loadCatalog, loadFaintStars,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";

illuminanceFromMagnitude(-1.46); // 9.89e-6 lux: Sirius
illuminanceFromMagnitude(6.5); // 6.47e-9 lux: the naked eye's limit
const catalog = await loadCatalog(); // 9,093 stars, 89 KB
const faintStars = await loadFaintStars(); // 108,883 more, 579 KB
const pass = await createStarsPass(device, { format, catalog, faintStars });
pass.update({ faintStars: 20_000 }); // the brightest of them, to magnitude 7.6
```

## The colors of the stars

Stars have colors, faint to the eye and plain to a camera: Betelgeuse is orange, Vega blue-white. A catalog carries the
difference as the color index, B−V: the star's magnitude through a blue filter less its magnitude through a yellow-green
one. Magnitudes count backwards, so a larger index is a redder star: 0 for Vega, 0.65 for the Sun, 1.85 for Betelgeuse.

Three steps turn the index into a color. A star radiates close to a black body, so two filters are enough to tell its
temperature, by Ballesteros' formula <Cite id="ballesteros2012" />: 10,100 K for Vega, 5,800 K for the Sun, 3,300 K for
Betelgeuse. A black body's temperature fixes its <Term of="Chromaticity">chromaticity</Term>, a point on the
<Term of="Planckian locus">Planckian locus</Term>, which Kang and colleagues fitted from 1,667 K to 25,000 K
<Cite id="kang2002" />. And a chromaticity becomes a color in linear sRGB, scaled so that its luminance is 1: 0.87, 0.99,
1.44 for Vega, 1.11, 0.98, 0.91 for the Sun, 1.62, 0.88, 0.36 for Betelgeuse. The star's lux then multiply it, so color
and brightness never mix. `colorFromColorIndex` does the three steps, and the shipped catalog holds the result for every
star in place of the index.

No star is green, and the steps say why: run them over every temperature the fit covers, and green is never the
strongest of the three channels. The locus runs from red through orange and white to blue and passes green by. All of
the catalog's colors lie inside sRGB's gamut, the reddest at 2,200 K, the bluest at 16,600 K. The catalog lists no color
index for 310 of its stars, and those get the typical one of their spectral type; the Hipparcos stars carry their index,
and the pass looks their color up in a table of 512, one per hundredth.

```ts
import {
    colorFromColorIndex, planckianLocus, temperatureFromColorIndex,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";

const kelvin = temperatureFromColorIndex(1.85); // 3,333 K: Betelgeuse
const [x, y] = planckianLocus(kelvin); // 0.415, 0.396: orange
// The three steps in one: linear sRGB whose luminance is 1.
const color = colorFromColorIndex(1.85); // [1.62, 0.88, 0.36]
```

These colors are saturated on purpose. They are what a camera records; what the eye makes of them at night is the
business of [the eye at night](#the-eye-at-night).

Run the three steps over every index from −0.3 to 2, and the color slides along the locus from blue-white through white
to orange (<Fig id="colorindex" />). White itself is a choice: linear sRGB calls D65 white, the CIE's daylight at 6,504
K, and the CIE's other standard whites lie close to the locus, A, a tungsten lamp, on it at 2,856 K, and the daylights
D50 to D75 a little above it, towards green <Cite id="cie2018" />. Jensen's simpler temperature agrees with Ballesteros'
within 5% only from 0.44 to 0.97, around the Sun; towards both ends the two [part ways](#precise-and-approximate). Under
the diagram each index is shown in its color by either formula, scaled so that its strongest channel is full, with Vega,
Polaris, Sirius and the Sun on the scale.

<ColorIndex />

Sorted by their index, the catalog's stars fall into two crowds, blue-white around 0 and orange around 1, with a gap
between them where the Sun would stand (<Fig id="star-colors" />). Only one star in 27 lies between 0.6 and 0.8, as
yellow as the Sun, and one in eight is redder than 1.4. Add the fainter Hipparcos stars, and the gap fills: one in ten
of them lies between 0.6 and 0.8. The bars hold the 9,093 stars of the Yale catalog, 116,837 with the Hipparcos stars
and 2.5 million with Tycho-2's, each catalog without its stars that have no measured index. Tycho-2's index is converted
from its own two bands <Cite id="esa1997" />, and for its typical star, of magnitude 11, it is uncertain by about 0.2,
four of the bins.

## Points of light

A star has no size, but its image has. No lens brings light to a perfect point: the eye's spreads a star into a spot
about an arcminute across, a camera's into a few pixels, and a screen cannot show anything smaller than one of its own.
The image an optical system makes of a point is its <Term of="Point spread function">point spread function</Term>, and a
renderer has to choose one.

sternenheer's is a Gaussian with a standard deviation of 0.7 pixels, `psfPixels`, some 1.6 pixels across at half its
height, and never under 0.6. The floor is there for a reason. Drawn narrower, a star falls between the pixels: at 0.4
pixels its pixels add up to a sixth more or less light depending on where it sits on the grid, and it flickers as the
sky turns. At 0.6 they add up to the star's light within a third of a percent, wherever it sits.

What a pixel then holds is a luminance in cd/m²: the star's lux over the solid angle of its spot on the sky. That solid
angle shrinks as the view zooms in, so a star does not grow with the magnification. It brightens, by the square of the
zoom, as it does in a telescope, while the Milky Way and the sky, spread over area, stay as they are. In a view of the
whole sky, 180° down 1,080 pixels, Sirius peaks at 0.4 cd/m², over a thousand times the dark sky around it.

No magnitude limit decides which stars show. The exposure does, as it does for the eye: eleven stops below display white
a star's image is cut off, `threshold`, which is 8.3 magnitudes, and a star that much fainter than one that reaches
white is not drawn at all. For a chart there is `magnitudeLimit` all the same. Set the exposure of <Fig id="stars" /> by
hand and open it stop by stop: the stars come up in order of their magnitudes, the faint ones last.

A screen shows nothing brighter than its white. A Gaussian that passes it burns out into a disc, wider the brighter the
star, until Sirius is a white coin: the classic mistake, and nothing an eye ever saw. sternenheer keeps the core a
point. Its exposed luminance stops at a knee, display white by default, `knee`, and the light beyond goes into a halo
around the star instead. The core whitens as far as its light passes the knee, as a sensor's channels fill up one after
the other, and the star's color lives on in the halo.

The halo moves that light and adds none: summed, its pixels hold what the knee took from the core, and `halo` says how
much of it they show, all of it by default. It falls with the cube of the distance from the star, as CIE's equation has
the glare a bright point throws in the eye <Cite id="cie2002" />, over a width set in your own field of view, so it is
as wide on any screen. Held against three photographs, the width that fits them best is a tenth of an arcminute, so
narrow that a halo is a glow of a few pixels that widens with a star's light alone. Only its display is shaped: it
brightens with the logarithm of its light, so a star a hundred times brighter gets a wider halo, not a burnt-out one. A
glow around a point is how a picture says bright: it is what makes a light look brighter than the white of the screen
<Cite id="spencer1995" />. The halo is a sign for it, the light spread rather than shown.

Zoom into <Fig id="stars" />: more stars come up, the ones already there grow their halos, and every core stays a point.
Switch the figure's `glare` off, and every star shrinks to its spot. The eye's own <Term of="Veiling glare">veiling
glare</Term>, CIE's equation <Cite id="cie2002" />, is a parameter of the pass too, `glare`, by the angle the picture
spans at your eye, but off by default: at the angle of a pixel it counts the scatter of the viewer's eye twice, once in
the picture and once in the eye that looks at it, and swells every star into a blob.

```ts
pass.update({
    psfPixels: 0.7, // the core's width, the standard deviation in pixels
    knee: 1, // where a core stops brightening, in units of display white
    halo: 1, // how much of the light beyond the knee its halo shows
    magnitudeLimit: 6.5, // only for a chart: the exposure decides otherwise
});
```

Together, these steps take one row of the catalog to a few pixels, and two more lie on the way, which later sections
explain: [the sky's turning](#the-sky-turning) puts the star in its place tonight, and [the air](#through-the-air)
lifts, dims and reddens it near the horizon. Each step comes with its numbers and the function that computes it, and the
stars to follow are ones that films made famous, from Vega in Contact to Canopus in Dune (<Fig id="onestar" />). Open
the exposure stop by stop, and the core's peak stays at the knee while the halo takes a larger share of the light.

Where a star's light goes past the knee can be measured: the pass draws a single star as readily as nine thousand,
hands its pixels back, and tells for each star what its core and its halo show, `readStars`. Vega, in the view of the
whole sky at a moonless night's exposure, peaks at some 90 times display white, the knee leaves its core about a
hundredth of its light, and the halo takes the rest (<Fig id="loupe" />). With the halo's curve undone, the pixels hold
that light to a tenth of a percent, on a target 129 pixels wide as on one of 1,025. On the curve, as a screen shows
them, the 33 by 33 pixels around the star hold a tenth of Vega's light. Below the knee the sums hold, for a star of
magnitude 6 to within a twentieth of a percent. Switch the `knee` off, and the pixels add up to all of Vega's light again, but a screen clips
them at its white and shows a sixteenth of it, in a burnt-out disc. Zoom in, and fainter stars reach the knee, a
magnitude and a half fainter for every doubling, while a dark sky behind them stays as it was.

## The eye at night

To the naked eye nearly every star is white. A handful of the brightest show a tint, Betelgeuse its orange, Vega a cold
blue-white, and the rest none, though a photograph finds color in all of them. The eye is what leaves it out. It sees
with two kinds of cells: cones, which need light and see color, and <Term of="Scotopic vision">rods</Term>, which need
very little and see none. From a few cd/m² down the rods join in, and below some hundredth of one they see alone
<Cite id="larson1997" />. A dark sky, at 0.0003 cd/m², lies far below that.

Two things follow, and they are easy to mix up. The first is about brightness. The rods are most sensitive to blue-green
light, the cones to yellow-green, so as the light fades, what is blue holds its brightness and what is red goes dark:
the <Term of="Purkinje shift">Purkinje shift</Term>. It reaches the stars too. Of two stars of the same magnitude, one
as blue-white as Vega and one as orange as Betelgeuse, the rods get twice the light from the blue one, by the fit to the
rods' sensitivity that helldunkel uses <Cite id="larson1997" />. The second is about color. The rods' picture has none,
yet a night does not look gray to us, it looks bluish, and painters have always given it blue. Jensen and colleagues put
that into their night sky as a blue shift <Cite id="jensen2001" />: whatever the rods see is given one pale blue.

helldunkel's `darkAdaptedEye` does both, and the pass applies it with `darkAdapted`, the figure with its `night vision`
switch. What the pass does is simpler than the eye, though, and worth knowing. It runs over the composed picture, stars,
Milky Way and sky together, pixel by pixel, before the exposure, and decides by that pixel's luminance how much of it
the rods see. For the sky and the Milky Way that is right: they are surfaces, and their luminance is what the eye meets.

For a star it is a stand-in. A star's pixel holds its lux over the solid angle of its spot, and that depends on the zoom
and on the width of its image, not on the star alone. In the view of the whole sky from the last section, every star
fainter than magnitude 2.5 falls below a hundredth of a cd/m² and turns wholly the rods' blue, and Sirius keeps two
thirds of its color. Zoom in ten times, and stars down to magnitude 7.5 keep some of theirs.

The eye does not work that way. Whether a star shows its color depends on whether its light at the eye is enough for the
cones, a matter of its lux, however a picture of it is zoomed. A fade by each star's illuminance would be closer to
the eye; sternenheer keeps one rule for every pixel instead. So read the switch as the night's blue cast: right for
the sky and the band, a rough guess for the points. Switch it off, and the stars show the colors of their
temperatures, as a camera records them. Switch it on and zoom in on a bright star, and its color returns as its pixels
brighten.

```ts
import { darkAdaptedEye } from "@himmelszelt/helldunkel";

// Linear sRGB in cd/m², as the eye sees it at that level: a patch of the
// Milky Way at 0.0003 cd/m² comes out blue, whatever its color was.
const seen = darkAdaptedEye([0.0003, 0.0003, 0.00025]);
// In the pass: the composed picture, before the exposure.
const pass = await createStarsPass(device, {
    format, catalog, darkAdapted: true,
});
```

Followed from daylight down to starlight, the blue shift takes the colors in turn (<Fig id="night-blue" />). Above
4 cd/m² the eye sees as a camera records; by a hundredth of one the cones are gone, the orange stars a stop darker
than the blue-white ones, and every color the same pale blue. The full Moon's sky lies below that already, at some
0.004 cd/m² <Cite id="puschnig2014" />, so in Jensen's model the blue of a moonlit night is the eye's, not the
sky's. A star's core lies wherever the zoom puts it, and its dot climbs out of the rods' range as the view zooms in: the
pass' shortcut, made visible. Lower the luminance to the full Moon's sky, and the white and the moonlit sky turn the
same blue.

## The sky turning

A catalog gives a star's place for one moment, the standard equinox of 2000, in equatorial coordinates, and the sky has
moved since. Earth turns under the stars once a sidereal day, which is sternzeit's <Term of="Sidereal time">sidereal
time</Term>, and its axis slowly turns too, the <Term of="Precession">precession</Term> of the equinoxes, some 50
arcseconds a year along the ecliptic <Cite id="meeus1998" />: since 2000 Vega has drifted 11 arcminutes from its catalog
place, a third of the Moon's width, more than a pixel of any view of the whole sky. The stars' own motion, their
<Term of="Proper motion">proper motion</Term>, is far smaller: the fastest star in the catalog, Groombridge 1830,
crosses a Moon's width in 260 years.

sternzeit does the astronomy, as it does for the Sun and the Moon: `stars.apparentPosition` moves one star by its
proper motion and precesses it to the date, per Meeus' chapter 21, and `stars.horizontalMatrix` folds the precession and
the sidereal time at your place into one rotation from the catalog's frame into your east, north and up. The pass takes
that rotation and turns nine thousand stars by it on the GPU, each one moved by its own proper motion first. Step the
moment a day at a time in <Fig id="stars" />, and the whole sky turns a degree, four minutes earlier each night. A y-up
engine takes the directions as `(x, z, -y)`.

```ts
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, stars } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const time = fromDate(new Date());
const observer = { latitude, longitude };
// One rotation for the whole catalog: precession and sidereal time in one.
const skyRotation = stars.horizontalMatrix(time, observer);
// The stars' own motion is per star: the years since J2000.0.
const years = (julianEphemerisDay(time) - 2451545) / 365.25;
```

## Through the air

The air does three things to a star's light, all of them worst near the horizon. It bends the light, so every star
shows higher than it is: on the horizon by some 35 arcminutes, more than the Moon's width, and a star that is truly
on the horizon already stands half a degree up. The pass takes the air as dunstkreis' table over the apparent altitude,
`airTable`, traced through the same air the sky is drawn from, one small texture per height of the observer, its
entries crowded towards the horizon, where the bend changes fastest. It lifts every star by it, looks the Milky Way up
along the unbent ray, and draws nothing below the lowest ray that still reaches space, which from the ground lies a
little below the geometric horizon and from orbit far below it. Near the horizon the air also squeezes the sky, as it
flattens the setting Sun: two stars show closer together than they are, and a star's light is spread thinner, by a
fifth on the horizon. A surface such as the Milky Way keeps its brightness as it is squeezed, so only the stars are
dimmed by it.

It takes light away, more of the blue: at the zenith the air lets 87% of the green through and 77% of the blue, at 5° up
a quarter of the green and a sixteenth of the blue, on the horizon some 4% of the red and next to none of the blue,
which is why a rising star is red and faint and why the Milky Way fades into the haze long before the horizon. The same
table holds dunstkreis' `airTransmittance` at every altitude, and the pass dims each star and each pixel of the Milky
Way by it.

And it twinkles. The air's turbulence bends a star's light this way and that on its way down, and a pupil seven
millimeters wide catches now more of it, now less. The patches of light and shade the turbulence throws on the ground
are some 6 cm wide, far wider than the pupil, so the eye gets no averaging from its aperture, and from one millisecond
to the next a star at the zenith flickers by a quarter of its light in ordinary air <Cite id="dravins1998" />. The eye
is slow, though: it adds the light up over a tenth of a second, some thirty flickers, and what is left is 8% at the
zenith, too little to notice, 17% at 30° up and two fifths 10° up, where the light passes through more of the
turbulence and the flicker slows down <Cite id="osborn2015" />. The model is dunstkreis', [its
chapter](../dunstkreis/) tells it, and its `scintillationIndex` gives the number for a seeing and an exposure, a
camera's as well as the eye's. Low down the air also disperses the star's light into a tiny spectrum, and once the red
and the blue pass through different patches they twinkle apart, which is the flashing of colors a low Sirius is famous
for. The pass takes the seeing as `seeing`, and draws the star's light with a random factor per channel, a new one
about every tenth of a second and smooth in between, correlated across the channels high up and independent low down,
from a hash of the star and the time: switch the figure's `twinkle` on and watch the horizon. A planet, a disc rather
than a point, twinkles far less, and sternenheer has none yet.

The sky behind the stars is dunstkreis': by day it outshines all of them, by moonlight the fainter ones, and the figure
meters on it where it is brighter than the stars' own light. What the eye makes of such a night was the subject of [the
eye at night](#the-eye-at-night).

How one star's flicker changes with the altitude, the seeing, the lens and the exposure, and why a planet's disc hardly
flickers, you can try in [dunstkreis' chapter](../dunstkreis/#flicker), next to the table of the air the pass takes.

## Lines between the stars

Nobody ever saw a line in the sky, and nobody finds their way around it without them. What is official about the
constellations is less than one would think. The International Astronomical Union agreed on a list of 88 in 1922 and on
their boundaries in 1928, areas that cover the whole sky between them, and it defines no figures at all
<Cite id="iauconstellations" />. The lines that join a constellation's stars into a shape are convention.

sternenheer ships one set of them, the figures of [d3-celestial](https://github.com/ofrohn/d3-celestial): 743 lines,
each between two stars of the catalog, in 4 KB. `loadConstellations` brings them, the pass takes them as
`constellations`, and its parameter of the same name picks which to draw, by their abbreviations: `["Ori"]`, the
exported `ZODIAC`, or `null` for all 88.

A line is a hint on the display, not light in the sky. It keeps its muted blue gray whatever the exposure, stops a few
pixels short of each star so the star stays the brighter thing, follows its two stars as the air lifts them, and shows
only while both stand above the horizon. Switch `lines` on in <Fig id="stars" /> and choose the zodiac: it has thirteen
constellations, not twelve, since the Sun also passes through Ophiuchus, which the calendar's signs leave out.

```ts
import {
    createStarsPass, loadConstellations, ZODIAC,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";

const constellations = await loadConstellations();
const pass = await createStarsPass(device, {
    format, catalog, constellations,
});
// By abbreviation: Orion and the Great Bear, the zodiac, or null for all.
pass.update({ constellations: ["Ori", "UMa"] });
pass.update({ constellations: ZODIAC, constellationOpacity: 0.2 });
```

## The Milky Way

Most of the night's starlight comes from stars too faint to see one by one, and together they are the band across the
sky. sternenheer draws them from NASA's Deep Star Maps of 2020 <Cite id="svs4851" />: the whole sky as a picture, made
by plotting 1.7 billion stars, most of them from Gaia's catalog, in the same J2000 equatorial coordinates as the catalog
here, so the one rotation turns both. osgHimmel drew a photograph of the Milky Way as a cube map with an intensity set
by hand <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />. This map is made of counted stars, and so it can be given a unit.

NASA publishes the map in layers, and which one a renderer takes is not a detail. One layer holds every star. Another is
the background alone, without the stars of the Hipparcos and Tycho catalogs, which reach down to magnitude 11.5.
sternenheer takes the background, because the pass draws the bright stars itself, as points in lux. A map that still
held them would show each of them twice: once as the pass' sharp point, and once more as a blob a texel wide baked into
the picture, 5 arcminutes in the large map and 10 in the small, a sixth and a third of the Moon's width.

And the baked one behaves like a patch of sky, not like a star. Its light is a luminance, so it does not brighten as the
view zooms in; it spreads into a blur. It never twinkles, has neither knee nor halo, and stays when the stars are
switched off. The air does treat it as it treats the whole map, pixel by pixel, so it rises and reddens near the horizon
as it should. But its light is counted twice, which undoes the calibration that follows.

Even the background has points left, the brightest of the stars it does hold, and the script that makes the shipped maps
takes those out too. Every texel more than 2.5 times as bright as the median of the 5 by 5 around it comes down to that
median, 487,205 of the map's 8.4 million, one in seventeen. What it lost is spread around it, over half a degree, so the
map keeps its light and loses its points.

Drawn under the same stars, either side of a line, sternenheer's map and NASA's show the difference: over the starred
layer every bright star appears twice, a point over a blur, and the band carries the stars' light once more (<Fig
id="starred" />). Switch the stars off, and the starred map keeps its own. Pick NASA's starless layer instead, as
published, and zoom in: the points the script takes out, a few hundred around Deneb, stay in it as faint blurred dots
under the drawn stars. NASA's layers are 4096 texels around, a texel 5 arcminutes, and sternenheer's map as fine, or
8192 once a pixel spans less than that. The starred layer is drawn at twice the light a texel, which matches its
background to sternenheer's, and its Deneb keeps about a twentieth of its light: NASA clipped the layer at 1, scaled it
for looks and spread each star over a texel.

<Starred />

A picture needs a unit before it can stand next to stars in lux. Masana and colleagues measured the light of all the
stars together, the <Term of="Integrated starlight">integrated starlight</Term>, from the same Gaia data: at the zenith
of a place at 40° north it averages a <Term of="Surface brightness">surface brightness</Term> of 23.2 magnitudes per
square arcsecond over a year, a fifth of the natural night sky's light <Cite id="masana2021" />. That zenith sweeps the
sky around declination 40°, and along the band from 35° to 45° the script adds up what the package draws: the catalog
gives a third of Masana's light, the Hipparcos stars a quarter, and the map is scaled to give the rest. A texel of value
1 then stands for 0.0012 cd/m², `MILKY_WAY_LUMINANCE`. That puts the map's brightest patch, in Sagittarius, a few
degrees from the galactic center, at 20.3 magnitudes per square arcsecond, three times as bright as a dark sky's 21.5,
and the galactic poles near 26, two hundred times fainter than the patch. A renderer that leaves the Hipparcos stars out
draws that band a quarter short. A sky designed rather than measured can brighten or dim the map by stops,
`milkyWayCompensation`, whose 0 is the calibrated light.

The band you see in <Fig id="stars" /> is the band you see on a dark night, at the light the sky gives it: zoom out and
dim the exposure, and it fades into the sky before the stars do.

A scene that needs no more than a backdrop, with no stars of its own, can do without the pass. The layer with every star
in it is the picture for that, and [rundbild](../rundbild/) the package: its `createCubeFromPanoramaPass` turns such a
panorama into a cube map, with an `azimuthOffset` of −90 laying the cube's axes along the catalog's, and a renderer
samples it along its ray turned back into that frame, by the transpose of `stars.horizontalMatrix`. NASA publishes the
layers as OpenEXR, to be converted first. Those stars are then pixels of a picture, with everything just said of the
baked ones.

A sky drawn from numbers can be held against the sky itself, as cameras record it. Six photographs carry all the check
needs, the moment, the place, the lens and the exposure: the Milky Way over Idaho and stars over the Watzmann through
wide lenses, Orion through a normal one, a night under a waning Moon in New Zealand, twilight over Slovenia, and the
Pleiades through a long lens (<Fig id="photos" />). Nothing in the figure is placed by hand. A script finds the stars in
each photograph, names them from the catalog, and fits where the camera pointed, how it was turned, its focal length and
its lens' distortion to as many of them as it names, from 85 to 796, and resamples each photograph to a lens without
distortion. What is left between the photograph's stars and the catalog's is 0.1 to 3.4 arcminutes, a pixel of these
pictures at most: the sky's rotation from sternzeit and the air's lift from dunstkreis put every star where the camera
found it, down to 4° over the horizon. The exposure is the camera's own, its f-number, seconds and ISO as one
EV<sub>100</sub>, less the stops by which each photograph's stars outshine that: 0.4 for a JPEG straight from the
camera, no more than a camera's ISO rating leaves open, within a stop for three more, edited as they are, and 2.3 and
3.2 for the two that were processed hard.

<Photos />

Past the stars' places, the script measures both pictures alike, and three things hold. A star's light in lux lands on
the pixels as a sensor records it. The Milky Way, calibrated to the measured starlight and never to a photograph, adds
within 0.4 magnitudes of what two of the photographs show against their own stars, and a magnitude more than the
Watzmann's. And moonlit air comes out blue, as in the photograph, from nothing but the Moon's light and the air. Three
things do not hold. The sky between the stars is 4 to 65 times darker than in three of the photographs: the air glows by
itself at night, which with the sunlit dust between the planets is most of the natural night sky's light <Cite
id="masana2021" />, it scatters the light of towns besides, and neither sternenheer nor dunstkreis draws any of that
yet. In so dark a sky the stars show one to four magnitudes fainter than a camera finds them, and the Milky Way stands
out four to five times as strongly. A bright star's halo is drawn four and two times as wide as Antares' in the two
processed photographs, and a third as wide as Rigel's in the one of Orion: no single width matches all three, so the
pass keeps the narrowest, and two of the photographs widen their stars by their processing more than any halo would. And
four of the photographs hold something sternenheer has no data for: Saturn, Mars, Venus, the Orion Nebula. Drag the line
across Orion and pick the last catalog button, Tycho-2: the photograph's faintest stars fill in, and many more besides.

## Twelve stops in eight bits

The Milky Way is all shadow detail. In the map more than half of the sky lies over six <Term of="Stop">stops</Term>
below the brightest value, under a sixtieth of it, the galactic poles eight stops, and only a thirtieth of the sky comes
within three. And a night's exposure opens until exactly those dark parts fill the screen.

The obvious file for a picture is 8-bit sRGB, and here it is the wrong one. sRGB spends its 256 levels where a screen
needs them by day: 68 on the brightest stop, 16 on the sixth, 9 on the eighth, where the poles lie, 3 on the tenth. At
the poles one level is 9% brighter than the one before. Rounding alone would hardly show, since the map's own grain
hides steps of that size, but a file this large is compressed, and WebP's compression errs by a level or two in blocks
of 8 by 8 texels. Where a level is 9%, that is a blotch of color, and an exposure for the poles shows it plainly.

So the shipped maps store each channel's logarithm. A byte counts 255 equal steps down 12 stops, `MILKY_WAY_STOPS`, each
3.3% above the last: 21 levels to every stop, the twelfth as many as the first, and 0 for black. The darkest step is a
four thousandth of the brightest, 29 magnitudes per square arcsecond in the map's calibration, two stops below the
darkest sky in the map. The shader undoes it, two to the power of what it reads, and as the GPU's filter blends the
stored numbers, the map is interpolated in stops, between two texels along their ratio. The file stays what a browser
reads by itself, a WebP, and the compression's error stays a few percent in every stop: 4 MB at 4096 by 2048 texels, 0.8
MB at half that.

The price is a quarter more bytes, 4.0 MB against sRGB's 3.2, and it is paid at the bright end too. In the top four
stops sRGB's steps are finer than 3.3%, and a ninth of the sky lies there, the hearts of the star clouds. From the sixth
stop down the logarithm has more levels, and more by comparison with every stop.

What NASA publishes is finer than either: OpenEXR in half floats, 1,024 levels to every stop. It is also 36 MB for the
same 4096 by 2048 texels, nine times the WebP, and no browser reads it without a decoder shipped along. A 16-bit PNG of
it comes to 39 MB. On the GPU the gap is smaller: half floats take 8 bytes a texel, 67 MB for the large map, the
logarithm's bytes 4, 34 MB.

```ts
import {
    createMilkyWay, loadMilkyWay, MILKY_WAY_MAPS,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";

// The large map, for zooming in: 4096 texels around, 4 MB.
const milkyWay = await loadMilkyWay(device, MILKY_WAY_MAPS.large);
// Or a map of your own in sRGB, stops 0, and what its white is in cd/m².
const own = createMilkyWay(device, bitmap, { luminance: 1e-3, stops: 0 });
```

Cut from the whole map three ways, the floats as the reference and the two 8-bit files as the package ships them, WebPs
at quality 95 decoded as `createMilkyWay` reads them, a patch around the north galactic pole shows the difference. At
its meter reading, magnified, the sRGB patch breaks into blotches of color, while the logarithm's stays as smooth. Make
it eight times as bright, and the steps stand out; switch the `WebP` off, and the sRGB patch, rounded alone, shows how
little of it is the rounding (<Fig id="store-dark" />). The sizes under the patches are the whole map's, at 2048 and
4096 texels around; NASA publishes no EXR at 2048, so that one is computed.

## A frame, pass by pass

How the pass turns all of this into pixels is the business of this section; a reader after the sky can skip to [where
the data comes from](#the-data-and-its-scripts).

A night sky is a strange picture to draw. Nearly every pixel is nearly empty, and what light there is sits in points,
the brightest a million times the faintest. The GPU's own ways of drawing a point fit it badly. A point primitive in
WebGPU is one pixel, with no size to set: a star drawn so hops from pixel to pixel as the sky turns, and cannot carry a
halo. A quad per star, the usual answer, costs by its area, and the areas could not differ more: a faint star needs a
few pixels, the halo of Sirius reaches across the picture, and every quad is blended into a float target on top of the
others. sternenheer turns the question around. It asks for every pixel which stars reach it, in compute passes
throughout, like dunstkreis and nachtgestirn, and writes into a texture you own.

The first pass runs once per star, `wgsl.bin`. It moves the star by its proper motion, turns it into your sky, lifts it
by the air, and drops it if it stands below the horizon, outside the picture or fainter than the magnitude limit. It
projects the star with the camera of dunstkreis' sky, bent projections included, and multiplies its lux by its color, by
what the air lets through and by the twinkling. From the width of the core and the pixels a degree of sky spans at that
spot follows the luminance at the star's center, and from the exposure how far out its image stays above the cut-off:
under three pixels with the defaults, and nowhere for a star too faint for the exposure, whose part in the frame ends
there.

What is left goes into tiles. The target is cut into squares of 16 by 16 pixels, `tileSize`, and every star counts
itself into each tile its image reaches: one as a rule, two near an edge, four at a corner. Thousands of stars do so at
once, so each tile's counter is raised atomically. A second step then writes the stars' numbers into their tiles. A
tile holds 64 stars, `capacity`, at four bytes each, 2 MB for a target of 1920 by 1080. Where more reach a tile than it
holds, its stars are sorted in by their light: each slot keeps the brighter of what it held and what arrives and hands
the other on, so a full tile keeps its brightest stars, the same ones every frame, in whatever order the stars came.
That costs a little, so the capacity should still cover the fullest tile: the figure takes 256, for the faint stars
along the Milky Way in a wide view.

The same pass looks after the light beyond the knee. For a star that passes it, that light is added as one point to a
second, coarser grid: half the target's size, with 64 pixels of margin around it, so a bright star just outside the
picture still throws its glow in. The point is shared among the four texels around it by how close each lies, so the
halo glides with the star instead of hopping. Adding has to be atomic again, and WGSL's atomics are integers, so the
pass keeps each float as its bits and swaps them in until no other star got in between. A small pass turns the grid into
a texture of levels, as a bloom does <Cite id="jimenez2014" />: it halves the grid level by level, each texel the mean
of the four below it, and adds the levels back up from the coarsest. Each level holds the light about twice as wide as
the one below and gets its share of it, for a profile that falls with the cube of the distance is a sum of Gaussians of
every width, and each level stands for those of its own. The shares add up to 1, so the halos move light and add none,
at the cost of blurring a half-size image whatever the number of stars: a target of 1920 by 1080 takes a grid of 1,024
by 604 texels, of which the default halo needs three levels, and `halo: 0` none, nor their memory.

The constellations' lines have a pass of their own, `wgsl.lines`. Each line is cut into eight pieces along the great
circle between its two stars, so it stays a curve where the projection bends, and each piece is put into the tiles it
crosses, sixteen to a tile, the way the stars are.

The last pass runs once per pixel, `wgsl.compose`. It finds the pixel's ray and asks whether it sees sky at all: above
the horizon, and not behind the Moon, whose disc the pass takes as `occluder`. Then it adds up, in cd/m²: the stars of
its tile, each a Gaussian around its center, capped at the knee; the halos, one lookup into the pyramid's sum; the Milky
Way, looked up along the ray as it ran before the air bent it, and dimmed by the air; the lines; and the background,
dunstkreis' sky. With `darkAdapted` the sum passes through [the eye at night](#the-eye-at-night). Last comes the
exposure, one multiplication by what the <Term of="EV100">EV100</Term> says, as in dunstkreis, then one of helldunkel's
tone curves, and for an 8-bit target a dither if you ask for it. One write per pixel, no blending, and no texture
besides yours and the halos' pyramid.

A renderer with more to draw over the stars does not want them tone mapped yet. With `toneMap: false` the pass writes
the light linear into a float target, and at an exposure of 1 that is cd/m², the next pass' background. The knee, the
cut-off and the halo are matters of the display, though, and need the exposure the picture is finally shown at:
`displayEV100`. The sky at the top is built this way, three passes in a row: dunstkreis' sky in cd/m², the stars over
it, and nachtgestirn's Moon over both, which alone exposes and tone maps. The passes `sky` and `moon` are made as their
chapters show:

```ts
// An exposure of 1: the pass writes cd/m², as it took them.
const UNIT_EV100 = -Math.log2(1.2);
const starsPass = await createStarsPass(device, {
    format: "rgba32float", toneMap: false, catalog, milkyWay,
});

// Per frame: the stars linear, their knee and halos by the display's EV100.
starsPass.update({ skyRotation, ev100: UNIT_EV100, displayEV100: ev100 });
sky.encode(encoder, skyTexture);
starsPass.encode(encoder, starsTexture, { background: skyTexture });
moon.encode(encoder, target, { background: starsTexture });
```

Everything is also there in pieces, for a renderer that wants the stars inside a pass of its own: the WGSL as strings,
`wgsl.bin`, `wgsl.compose` and the rest, and `paramsData`, which packs the parameters as the shaders read them.

A frame can also be taken apart. Made with `debug`, the pass keeps what each of its passes leaves for the next and hands
it out with `inspect`, and times every pass on the GPU's clock where the browser allows it. Drawn as a graph, a frame
reads from left to right: the catalogs, the lines and the maps go in, each dispatch runs over its stars, line pieces,
levels or pixels, and what it leaves rides on the arrow to the next, the tiles and how full each one is, the light past
the knee as the bin pass left it in the halos' grid, the halos as that grid spreads them, the tiles the lines cross, and
the frame they make (<Fig id="passes" />). The figure exposes for a moonless night, EV<sub>100</sub> −9, or for the sky
where the Sun or the Moon lights it brighter. Off, as by default, nothing of it is kept and nothing costs. Lower the
capacity, and the tiles along the Milky Way fill up and keep only their brightest stars; widen the tiles, and every
pixel has more stars to look through. Switch the Moon and the air off, and their passes dim in the graph.

What the tiles buy is easy to count. A pixel looks through the stars of its own tile, never more than `capacity`,
instead of every quad that covers it, and the halos, which would make the brightest quads as large as the picture, cost
it one lookup. Nothing is blended, so the target can be any format a compute pass writes, `rgba32float` too, which
WebGPU blends only with the optional feature `float32-blendable`.

## What it costs

A night sky is a backdrop: whatever else a frame shows stands in front of it, and a renderer has little time to spare
for what lies behind. The sky this chapter opens with, both catalogs with their halos and the Milky Way through the air,
takes sternenheer 0.14 ms on a target of 1920 by 1080 pixels, under a hundredth of the 16.7 ms a frame at 60 Hz has. It
holds 41 MiB on the GPU, less than three half float render targets of that size, and downloads 1.4 MiB.

The time does not go where the stars are. The catalog alone takes 0.04 ms, and the chapter's sky, with 118,000 stars,
their halos, the map and the air, only some three times that. Half of a frame is the last pass, which visits every
pixel, so the cost follows the target, not the sky: 3840 by 2160 pixels, four times as many, take three times as long.
Twinkling, the constellations' lines and the 4K map together add a seventh, and the larger map holds 32 MiB more
(<Fig id="costs" />).

<Costs />

Stars by the million are another matter. With the 2.4 million stars `loadDeepStars` adds, the same sky takes 0.65 ms,
over four times as long, seven tenths of it in the pass that adds their light. It holds twice the memory and downloads
nine times as much, which is one reason the package makes that catalog and does not ship it; [its
license](#the-data-and-its-scripts) is the other. How these times are taken, on the GPU's own clock with the cases in
turn, is told [with dunstkreis' costs](../dunstkreis/#what-it-costs); with `pnpm dev` running, `pnpm -C site bench
sternenheer` measures the same on your GPU.

## The data and its scripts

Every point in the picture is somebody's measurement, and none of them the package's own: three catalogs of stars, one
set of lines and one map, each from a public source. Each is kept as a file a person can read, the CSV files in the
repository and the largest catalog where its archive serves it, and turned into what the GPU reads by a script that
runs again with one command. The scripts are plain Node and live next to the source, in
[`packages/sternenheer/scripts`](https://github.com/cgcostume/himmelszelt/tree/main/packages/sternenheer/scripts). Each
prints the terms its data comes under and the credit they ask for, keeps its downloads in a cache, and does nothing
while what it wrote is newer than what it read. The catalogs leave them gzip compressed and turned into byte planes: the
first byte of every star, then the second of every star, and so on, so that neighbors' magnitudes and positions, which
differ little, lie side by side, and the loaders turn them back with the browser's `DecompressionStream`.

`pnpm data:catalog` reads `data/brightstars.csv`, the Yale catalog with a line per star, 374 KB: the six numbers of [Sirius'
row](#nine-thousand-stars). The magnitudes, positions and motions came by way of osgHimmel's source, the color indices
from the catalog's own file, and the 310 stars without one got the typical index of their spectral type. The script
turns the hours of right ascension into degrees, runs each index through [the three steps to a
color](#the-colors-of-the-stars) and writes `brightstars.bin.gz`, eight 32-bit floats a star, 208 KB, and
`brightstars-compact.bin.gz`, three 32-bit words a star, 89 KB: the magnitude, the color index and the motions as the
catalog lists them, the position within a sixth of an arcsecond, finer than the catalog's own rounding to about a
second. NASA's archive lists its copy of the catalog as a work of the U.S. Government, for public use
<Cite id="hoffleit1991" />.

`pnpm data:hipparcos` reads `data/hipparcos.csv`, 3.4 MB: the Hipparcos catalogue as NASA's HEASARC serves it
<Cite id="esa1997" />, cut down to what the Yale catalog lacks and sorted brightest first. A line holds the magnitude,
the position, moved from the catalogue's epoch of 1991.25 to 2000 by the star's own motion, and the color index, the
Sun's for the 1,139 stars without one. Left out are 263 entries without a position or a magnitude and 9,072 that are
Yale's stars again. The script writes the stars twice: two 32-bit words a star, brightest first, 701 KB, and 6 bytes a
star sorted into the deep stars' cells, 579 KB. ESA distributes the catalogue under a
Creative Commons license for non-commercial use, CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO, with the credit ESA, which is not the MIT license of
the package's code. So the npm package carries neither the file nor the CSV, and whoever wants the faint stars makes
them, accepting ESA's terms: outside the repository the script fetches the catalogue from HEASARC, 15 MB once, and cuts
it down the same way, to the same stars.

`pnpm data:tycho` makes the deep stars from the Tycho-2 catalogue <Cite id="hog2000" />, under the same terms: a download of
200 MB, once, from which it keeps the 2.4 million stars the two other files lack, sorts them into 384 cells of the sky,
brightest first in each, and writes them in full, 14.1 MB, and compact, 12.0 MB. No CSV is kept of it, for its size: the
catalogue as HEASARC serves it is the record.

So each catalog comes in two layouts, and the loaders fetch the compact one unless asked for `"full"`. A compact faint
star stands within 0.6 arcseconds of its full twin, a third of a pixel where the stars figure zooms in closest, half a
degree across 1080 pixels, and less than the median one has moved by its own motion since 2000, which neither layout
holds. Its direction comes from the cell and the place in it, a point on a cube's face made unit length, without a sine
or a cosine. The Yale file shrinks to less than half and the faint and the deep stars' by about a sixth; on the GPU the deep
stars hold 4.5 MiB less and the Hipparcos stars 0.4 MiB more, as the pass keeps a slot for every place of their cells'
blocks. Neither layout draws faster: the time goes to adding up light, not to reading stars. The
pass takes either layout for each catalog and builds its shaders for the ones it gets, so the decoder of the other costs
nothing.

`pnpm data:constellations` reads `data/constellations.csv`, 743 lines in 10 KB: a constellation's abbreviation and the two
rows of the star file its line joins. The figures are d3-celestial's, by Olaf Frohn, under the BSD 3-Clause license,
each of their points matched to the catalog's star at that place, all within half an arcminute. The script writes
triples of 16-bit numbers, 4 KB.

`pnpm data:milkyway` is the one that works for its result, and the one with needs: ImageMagick with OpenEXR and WebP, and a
download of 36 MB, once, into a cache. It fetches NASA's background layer, reads it as floats, takes the stars out and
spreads their light, adds up the two catalogs along the calibration's band and prints the scale, as [the Milky
Way](#the-milky-way) describes. Then it writes the two maps as WebP, with [the logarithm in 8
bits](#twelve-stops-in-eight-bits), the small one averaged down from the large in linear light, and `milkyway.json` with
the scale and what it was matched against. NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio asks for its credit, and for Gaia's.
With `--size 8k` it makes a larger map from NASA's own larger file, 137 MB, which the package does not ship: 19 MB, a
texel 2.6 arcminutes.

`pnpm assets` runs the scripts whose files are not in the repository, the two of ESA's catalogues and the 8K map, and
with `--all` the others too; at the repository's root it does so for every package, as the site's build does before it
starts. An npm package has no `pnpm` scripts, so the package brings the same as a command, which writes into the
installed package's `data` folder, where `loadFaintStars` and `loadDeepStars` look:

```sh
cd packages/sternenheer
pnpm assets               # the Hipparcos and the Tycho-2 stars, the 8K map: 352 MB
pnpm assets --all         # every file, the shipped ones too
pnpm data                 # all five below, in turn
pnpm data:catalog         # brightstars.csv to brightstars(-compact).bin.gz
pnpm data:hipparcos       # hipparcos.csv to hipparcos(-compact).bin.gz
pnpm data:tycho           # HEASARC's Tycho-2 to tycho(-compact).bin.gz
pnpm data:constellations  # constellations.csv to constellations.bin
pnpm data:milkyway        # NASA's EXR to the two maps and milkyway.json

# Where the package is installed from npm, accepting ESA's terms:
npx @himmelszelt/sternenheer data hipparcos tycho
```

What is not a script is the step before the CSV files: picking the columns out of the catalogs as their archives serve
them, and matching Hipparcos against Yale and d3-celestial's points against the catalog's stars. Those ran once,
`data/README.md` says what they did, and the CSV files are their record. To correct a star or bring a catalog of your
own, edit a CSV and run its script, or skip the files altogether: `catalogFromStars` builds a catalog in memory, full
by default and compact on request, and `packStars` the faint stars' full layout.

```ts
import {
    catalogFromStars, colorFromColorIndex,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternenheer";

// A catalog of your own, here of Vega alone: degrees, arcseconds a year,
// and a color.
const catalog = catalogFromStars([{
    magnitude: 0.03, rightAscension: 279.2346, declination: 38.7836,
    properMotionRightAscension: 0.202, properMotionDeclination: 0.286,
    color: colorFromColorIndex(0),
}]);
```

Which of the files a renderer loads depends on how far its views zoom in. A map pays off once a pixel spans less than a
texel of the next coarser one: across 1080 pixels, in a view narrower than 118° for the 4K map and 79° for the 8K map
(<Tab id="sizes" />). A catalog pays off once its stars show, and every doubling of the zoom reaches [a magnitude and a
half deeper](#nine-thousand-stars). At the stars figure's exposure for a moonless night, half of the Hipparcos stars
show in a view of 87°, but half of the Tycho-2 stars only in one of 29°; at EV<sub>100</sub> −9, as a long photograph
exposes, in views of 167° and 136°. So a wide view needs neither the 8K map nor Tycho-2, which together download 31 MB
and hold over 200 MiB on the GPU, and a zoomed view gains from both. The stars figure works that way: it fetches the 8K
map only once a pixel at the center of its view spans less than a texel of the 4K map, and draws it once it has arrived
(<Fig id="stars" />).

## Precise and approximate

Two pieces come in a precise and a cheaper variant. The precession is Meeus' three angles and their rotation, or
Jensen et al.'s linear approximation of them <Cite id="jensen2001" />, which sternzeit offers as `stars` under its
`approx` entry: within an arcsecond of each other over this century, far below a pixel, so the approximate one is
the one to take. The star's temperature is Ballesteros' formula or Jensen's, and here the difference shows: Jensen's
makes Vega 12,500 K instead of 10,100 and Betelgeuse 2,900 K instead of 3,300, which is bluer blue stars and redder
red ones, osgHimmel's colors. `colorFromColorIndex` and `colorFromColorIndexApprox` are both there, to recolor
a catalog either way.

## The people behind the numbers

Dorrit Hoffleit edited the Yale Bright Star Catalogue from its third edition, of 1964, to the fifth of 1991 with Wayne
Warren that the stars here come from <Cite id="hoffleit1991" />. The fainter ones are the European Space Agency's,
from the catalogue of its Hipparcos satellite, published in 1997 <Cite id="esa1997" />. Jean Meeus' chapter on
precession gives the three angles and the rotation sternzeit turns the catalog by <Cite id="meeus1998" />. Henrik Wann
Jensen and his colleagues put the stars into a renderer in lux in 2001, with the approximate precession and the
temperature formula osgHimmel took from them, and with the blue shift the eye at night still uses
<Cite id="jensen2001" />.

Fernando Ballesteros derived the temperature from the color index by treating a star as a black body seen through two
filters <Cite id="ballesteros2012" />, and Bongsoon Kang and his colleagues at Dong-A University and Samsung fitted the
Planckian locus that turns the temperature into a color, for televisions that change their white
<Cite id="kang2002" />. Greg Spencer and his colleagues worked out in 1995 why a bright point shows with a glow around
it, in the eye and in a picture, the reasoning behind the halo <Cite id="spencer1995" />, and Jorge Jimenez showed the
chain of blurs that spreads it <Cite id="jimenez2014" />. Gregory Ward Larson, Holly Rushmeier and Christine Piatko
fitted the rods' luminance to a color's three numbers in 1997, the brightness the eye at night goes by
<Cite id="larson1997" />.

Andrew Young worked out how the twinkling scales with the air, which Dainis Dravins and his colleagues measured over
three papers in the nineties <Cite id="dravins1998" />. The constellations' figures are Olaf Frohn's, from his
d3-celestial. The Milky Way's map is Ernie Wright's, of NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio <Cite id="svs4851" />,
and its calibration rests on Eduard Masana and colleagues' map of the natural night sky, which summed the light of
Gaia's stars <Cite id="masana2021" />.
