Before there were clocks, the sky was the clock: the Sun set the day, the Moon the month, the seasons the year. That is
why its motions can be predicted so well, forward or back, for any place and any moment. sternzeit computes the Sun's
and the Moon's part of them and leaves the stars to [sternenheer](../sternenheer/). It answers the way a renderer
asks: with the direction to point a light along, the angle to turn a crescent by, how brightly Earth lights the Moon's
night side, and how far an eclipse has come, as a number a shader can use. None of it is guesswork. Jean Meeus wrote
the recipes down <Cite id="meeus1998" />, and [SunCalc](https://github.com/mourner/suncalc),
[astronomia](https://github.com/commenthol/astronomia) and [Astronomy Engine](https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy)
compute them in JavaScript already; sternzeit is the one made for real-time rendering.

`@himmelszelt/sternzeit` is written in TypeScript and ships as typed ES modules without a single dependency. It touches
neither the DOM nor the GPU, so it runs anywhere JavaScript runs, in a worker or on a server too, and it is cheap enough
to call every frame: a few microseconds for the Sun or the Moon, where a frame at 60 Hz lasts more than 16,000.
Everything comes [twice](#precise-and-approximate), precise and approximate. What to do with the numbers is up to you:
give a CAD or architecture viewer the right light and shadows for a real site and hour, let a game's day follow the
player's clock, label what is up right now in augmented reality, or show the sky over a date in history. Four tasks are
worked out below, code included: [the golden hour](#the-sun) for any day and place, a crescent [turned the right
way](#the-moon), [the next eclipse](#eclipses), and [a moon calendar of your own](#a-month-of-moons).

All of them start from the same three bodies. Earth, tilted and turning, is where we watch from
(<Fig id="armillary-sphere" />), and its turning is what sweeps the sky around us once a day. It circles the Sun once a
year, and the tilt makes the seasons of that year. The Moon circles Earth about once a month, lit by sunshine and,
faintly, by <Term of="Earthshine">earthshine</Term>.

## Counting time the astronomer's way

Calendars are a mess. Months have different lengths, leap years come and go, and in 1582 ten days were simply skipped.
None of that belongs in orbital mechanics, so astronomers count days instead: the <Term of="Julian Day" /> is the
number of days since noon on January 1, 4713 BCE. It is a single, continuous number, fractional for the time of day.
Today is somewhere around 2.46 million.

Most formulas then don't count in days but in <Term of="Julian century">Julian centuries</Term> since
<Term of="J2000.0">J2000</Term>, noon on January 1, 2000. What changes slowly, such as the tilt of Earth's axis or the
shape of an orbit, is written as a polynomial in time, and counted in centuries its terms stay modest numbers instead of
tiny coefficients multiplied by millions of days.

Earth's rotation makes a fine clock, but not a perfect one. Tidal friction slows it, by an amount that varies over the
centuries, so time counted by the turning Earth, universal time or UT, falls behind
<Term of="Ephemeris time">ephemeris time</Term>, the uniform time in which the orbits are computed. The gap between the
two is <Term of="Delta T">ΔT</Term>: about a minute today, and hours in antiquity <Cite id="iers" />
<Cite id="espenak2006" /> (<Fig id="delta-t" />). In code, one rule covers every function. One that takes a moment, as
`fromDate` returns it, reads it as UT and adds ΔT itself. One that takes a bare Julian Day expects it in ephemeris time
already, and `julianEphemerisDay` gives you that. Mixed up, they misplace the Moon by a fiftieth of its width today, by
half in the year 1000.

<DeltaT />

Then there is <Term of="Sidereal time">sidereal time</Term>, which is Earth's rotation measured against the stars
rather than the Sun. The two differ, because Earth does not only turn, it also moves along its orbit, by almost a degree
a day: 360° in a year of 365 days. After one full turn against the stars, the Sun has shifted by that degree, and Earth
has to turn a little longer, about four minutes, to face it again. So a day by the stars lasts about 23 hours and
56 minutes, a day by the Sun 24 hours, and over a year the four minutes add up to one whole extra turn: Earth turns 366
times against the stars, but only 365 times from noon to noon. Sidereal time, θ, is that rotation as an angle. It is
what connects the sky's coordinates to your spot on the ground: the dashed meridian through your location rotates with
it (<Fig id="armillary-sphere" />), and from one day to the next, at the same clock time, it has gained almost a degree
(<Tab id="time" />).

In code, a moment is a JavaScript `Date` or the same moment spelled out, and each of these counts is one call away:

```ts
import {
    fromDate, fromJulianDay, julianDayUT, julianEphemerisDay, siderealTime,
    toDate,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Any JavaScript Date, e.g., new Date() for now. Only the instant
// counts: no position depends on the browser's time zone.
const time = fromDate(new Date("2026-09-21T20:00:00+02:00"));
// Or the same moment spelled out: 20:00 in Berlin (UTC+2).
const same = {
    year: 2026, month: 9, day: 21,
    hour: 20, minute: 0, second: 0, utcOffsetSeconds: 7200,
};

const jd = julianDayUT(same); // 2461305.25, the offset applied
const jde = julianEphemerisDay(time); // ΔT added, for the orbits
const theta = siderealTime(time); // Greenwich mean sidereal, in deg
const date = toDate(fromJulianDay(jd)); // and back to a Date
```

## Earth, the platform we observe from

Summer is not warm because Earth is closer to the Sun: it is closest in early January. Earth's rotation axis is tilted
against the plane of its orbit by about 23.4°, the
<Term of="Obliquity of the ecliptic">obliquity of the ecliptic</Term>, ε (<Fig id="orbits" />). In June the northern
half leans towards the Sun, which then stands higher and stays up longer there, so its light arrives steeper and for
longer; in December it leans away, and the southern half has it the other way round. Where the tilted equator crosses
the orbit's plane lies the <Term of="Vernal equinox">vernal equinox</Term>, the point where the Sun crosses the
equator northwards in March. Neither is quite fixed: the tilt drifts slowly over millennia, and the Moon's pull adds a
small nod, the <Term of="Nutation">nutation</Term>, which shifts the tilt and the equinox by under 20{" "}
<Term of="Arcsecond">arcseconds</Term>, 3600ths of a degree. That is a hundredth of the Moon's width, nothing a sky
dome shows, but the Moon covers it in half a minute, and drawn among the stars it would stand that far from its place
without it (<Tab id="earth" />).

## Coordinates on the sky

To say where something stands on the sky takes two angles, as longitude and latitude do on Earth, and every pair needs a
reference: a plane to measure from, and a zero on it. Astronomy has four such systems, and each exists because one step
of the computation is simplest in it. Two of them are built on the tilt and the vernal equinox. The equinox makes a
natural zero: it is one of the two points where the equator and the ecliptic cross, so it lies on both, and the Sun
marks it every March, no star needed. <Term of="Ecliptic coordinates">Ecliptic coordinates</Term> take the plane of
Earth's orbit, the ecliptic (<Fig id="armillary-sphere" />), as their equator. The Sun never leaves it, and the Moon and
the planets stay within a few degrees, so orbits are computed here: an ecliptic longitude, l, counted from the vernal
equinox, and a small latitude, β, off the plane.

<Term of="Equatorial coordinates">Equatorial coordinates</Term> take Earth's equator instead, carried out onto the sky
as the celestial equator (<Fig id="armillary-sphere" />): right ascension, α, along it, also counted from the vernal
equinox, and declination, δ, north or south of it. This is the sky's own map, the one star catalogs use, since a star
keeps both values while Earth turns underneath. The two systems, ecliptic and equatorial, differ only by the obliquity,
a tilt about the line towards the vernal equinox.

Both are <Term of="Geocentric">geocentric</Term>, and nobody stands at Earth's center. From the surface, a nearby body
appears slightly shifted by its <Term of="Parallax">parallax</Term>, π: not at all for the stars, by 8.8 arcseconds for
the Sun, and by up to a degree for the Moon, two of its own diameters. The topocentric position, α′ and δ′, corrects for
it, with the observer on the flattened ellipsoid and at their height above sea level.

<Term of="Horizontal coordinates">Horizontal coordinates</Term>, finally, are the observer's own: an altitude, h, above
the horizon and an azimuth, A, from north through east. They follow from the topocentric position through the
<Term of="Hour angle">hour angle</Term>, which sidereal time provides. The celestial pole, around which the sky turns,
stands above the horizon exactly as high as the place's latitude, φ (<Fig id="local-frame" />): at the North Pole,
latitude 90°, it stands straight overhead, and at the equator, latitude 0°, it lies on the horizon.

Whatever the steps between these systems need comes from just two things, the moment and the place
(<Fig id="pipeline-inputs" />). The moment gives the sidereal time and `jde`, the Julian Day in ephemeris time, both
first met in [counting time](#counting-time-the-astronomers-way). From `jde` follow the obliquity and the body's
parallax. All of these are the same for everyone on Earth. Only the place is your own: the observer, given by a
latitude, a longitude and, if you like, a height.

<Inputs />

A position moves through these systems one function at a time (<Fig id="coordinate-pipeline" />). The ecliptic and the
equatorial position are the same wherever you stand; the observer comes in only on the way to the topocentric one. You
rarely need to walk them yourself: each position also comes from a single call that covers the whole way there, and a
renderer mostly needs the last two, `horizontalPosition` and `direction`. The calls follow [the one rule from counting
time](#counting-time-the-astronomers-way): the first two need only the orbits and take `jde`, and from the topocentric
position on, where Earth's rotation comes in, they take the moment as `fromDate` returns it.

<Pipeline />

The last call is the one a renderer places and lights things by: `direction` returns the `horizontalPosition` as a unit
vector in the observer's east-north-up frame, <Term of="ENU">ENU</Term> for short, with x pointing east, y north and z
up; an engine with y up, as most real-time renderers have it, assigns it as `(x, z, -y)`. Refraction is left out on
purpose. The altitude is the geometric one, where the body stands, not where the air lifts it for the eye, at the
horizon by more than the Sun's own diameter. Bending the light that way is the job of an atmosphere renderer such as
[dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/), and `earth.atmosphericRefraction` gives an estimated lift where you need it.

## The Sun

Over a day, the Sun and the Moon cross your sky along a path, and the Sun followed through a year, always at the same
hour, traces an <Term of="Analemma">analemma</Term> (<Fig id="sun-path" />). The Moon's path, in contrast, never quite
closes, as it moves about 13° a day against the stars. The analemma's height swings with the seasons, from the tilt of
Earth's axis, and its sideways swing comes from the <Term of="Equation of time">equation of time</Term>: Earth moves
faster along its orbit in January than in July, and the tilt adds a second, twice-yearly wobble, so the Sun runs up to
about 16 minutes ahead of the clock in November and 14 behind it in February <Cite id="meeus1998" />. Where the
analemma's dots crowd, at the top and the bottom, the Sun lingers before it turns back: the solstices, Latin for the Sun
standing still.

<Controls height={false} />

Seen from Earth, the Sun circles the sky once a year along the ecliptic, against stars we cannot see by day. Computing
where it stands follows a pattern that most of this library repeats: a rough position, a corrected one, and the one we
see, named in the functions' prefixes `mean`, `true` and `apparent`. The mean one comes from a Sun moving
around the sky at a steady pace, just under a degree a day, as if Earth's orbit were a perfect circle: the
<Term of="Mean longitude">mean longitude</Term>. Orbits are ellipses, though, and the
<Term of="Equation of the center">equation of the center</Term> turns it into the
<Term of="True longitude">true longitude</Term>. Nutation and <Term of="Aberration">aberration</Term>, a small shift
caused by Earth's own orbital speed, then give the <Term of="Apparent position">apparent position</Term> in right
ascension and declination, and from there the conversions between the coordinate systems take it to the observer
(<Fig id="coordinate-pipeline" />).

Aberration hides a subtlety. Sunlight is 8 minutes 20 seconds old, but the delay matters only together with motion: at
30 km/s along its orbit, Earth carries us some 15,000 km sideways while the light is underway, so the Sun appears
20.5 arcseconds behind where it actually stands <Cite id="meeus1998" />. That is a ninetieth of the Sun's width,
invisible to the eye, but it moves the start and end of an eclipse by some 40 seconds. The true longitude leaves it
out, the apparent position adds it, and so `sun.direction` points where the Sun shows. For the Moon, 1.3 light seconds
away, the shift is under an arcsecond, and sternzeit leaves it out.

So far, the Sun is a direction: where it stands at any moment, and the paths it takes over a day and over a year. To
draw it, a renderer also needs its size, and the analemma's uneven loops already hinted that it changes: Earth moves
faster in January because it is closer to the Sun then (<Tab id="sun" />). Its orbit is almost a circle, at an
<Term of="Orbital eccentricity">eccentricity</Term> of about 0.0167 <Cite id="meeus1998" />, yet that puts Earth about
3% closer to the Sun in early January than in early July: `distance` shrinks, and `apparentAngularDiameter`, the width
of the Sun's disc, grows by as much.

With the Sun's altitude at hand, the library leaves out the conveniences on purpose: there is no `sunrise()`, and no
`dawn()` or `dusk()` either. Each is the moment the altitude crosses a threshold, so walking a day in one-minute steps
finds all of them, the start and end of the <Term of="Golden hour">golden</Term> and the
<Term of="Blue hour">blue hour</Term> too, or any other threshold you care to render.

```ts
import {
    fromDate, fromJulianDay, julianDayUT, sun, toDate,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Midnight by the Sun at your longitude, not at Greenwich's, so the
// day holds sunrise before sunset wherever you are.
const offset = longitude / 360;
const today = julianDayUT(fromDate(new Date())) + offset;
const midnight = Math.floor(today - 0.5) + 0.5 - offset;
const at = (minute: number) => fromJulianDay(midnight + minute / 1440);
// One altitude a minute, computed once for every threshold below.
const altitudes = Array.from({ length: 1441 }, (_, minute) =>
    sun.horizontalPosition(at(minute), { latitude, longitude }).altitude);

function crossings(deg: number): Date[] {
    const found: Date[] = [];
    for (let minute = 1; minute <= 1440; minute++) {
        if ((altitudes[minute - 1] > deg) !== (altitudes[minute] > deg)) {
            found.push(toDate(at(minute)));
        }
    }
    return found;
}

// The Sun is up once its refracted upper limb clears the horizon,
// the -0.833 degrees every almanac uses. Both stay undefined in the
// midnight sun or the polar night.
const [sunrise, sunset] = crossings(-0.833);
const [dawn, dusk] = crossings(-6); // civil twilight
```

## The Moon

Seen from Earth, the Moon circles the sky as well, not in a year but in a month: it rises some 50 minutes later every
day. It is also the hardest body to follow. It is close, its orbit is tilted against Earth's by about 5°, and the Sun
actually pulls on it about twice as hard as Earth does: a three-body problem, the original one, which has no exact
solution. Its position follows the Sun's pattern, a mean longitude corrected into the apparent one, but where the Sun
needs a single equation of the center, the Moon needs dozens of periodic terms, built from four angles, each turning at
its own pace. Being close also means a parallax of up to a degree, so the topocentric position matters most here
(<Tab id="moon" />).

<Controls height={false} />

Beyond the Moon's position, the table holds what a renderer needs to draw the Moon. Its size swings far more than the
Sun's: the Moon looks about 14% wider at its closest than at its farthest, the gap between a supermoon and a micromoon.
It always shows the same face, almost: its tilted orbit and varying speed let us peek a few degrees around its edges
over a month, the optical <Term of="Libration">libration</Term>, so that over time we see 59% of its surface.

Then there is its light. Half of the Moon is always in sunshine, and its phase is how much of that half we see. The
<Term of="Phase angle">phase angle</Term>, the angle Sun-Moon-Earth, measures it, and the
<Term of="Illuminated fraction">illuminated fraction</Term> turns that into what the phase looks like, from 0 at new
moon to 1 at full moon <Cite id="meeus1998" />. It is only a first measure of moonlight, though: a half moon gives about
a tenth of the full moon's light, not half, as the shadows of its craters grow longer away from full moon. For shading
the Moon itself, a renderer wants the light direction: `sunDirection` points from the Moon's center to the Sun, in ENU
like every other direction here. It differs from the Sun's direction seen from Earth by at most 0.16°, under a third of
the Sun's width, since the Moon stands 384,000 km off to the side.

The night side is not entirely dark, and not a hole either: no star has ever shone through the Moon. Earth reflects
sunlight too, and around new moon, when Earth seen from the Moon is almost full, that earthshine gives the unlit part of
a thin crescent a faint, ashen glow. How strong it is follows from Earth's phase as seen from the Moon
<Cite id="vandehulst1980" />, scaled by Earth's albedo <Cite id="jensen2001" />.

Even a Moon drawn without any shading needs one angle: the tilt of its crescent. Getting it wrong is a classic: games
and illustrations like to hang a crescent in the night sky with its lit side up. That cannot happen. The lit side always
faces the Sun, and a crescent stands less than 90° from it, so with its lit side up, the Sun would stand above it, and
above the horizon too: it would be day. Nor do the horns ever reach around the disc, as they do on a crescent cut out
with a smaller circle: the edge of the lit half is a circle on the sphere seen at a slant, a half-ellipse on the disc,
so its two tips always lie at the ends of one diameter, however thin the crescent gets.

The exact tilt is the <Term of="Position angle">position angle</Term> from the Moon to the Sun, measured on the sky from
north through east, and the <Term of="Parallactic angle">parallactic angle</Term> turns that from the sky's north to
your own up. `moon.brightLimbAngle` does both; the{" "}
<Term of="Position angle of axis">position angle of the axis</Term> turns the Moon's north pole, and with it its face,
the same way. The rest of the Moon's light is a call each:

```ts
import { fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, moon } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const time = fromDate(new Date());
const obs = { latitude, longitude };
// Functions of a bare Julian Day take ephemeris time.
const jde = julianEphemerisDay(time);
const lit = moon.illuminatedFraction(jde); // 0 new, 1 full
// Half a day on tells waxing from waning without a calendar.
const waxing = moon.illuminatedFraction(jde + 0.5) > lit;
// Counterclockwise from up, as the crescent stands in your sky.
const brightLimb = moon.brightLimbAngle(time, obs);
// For shading: from the Moon's center to the Sun, and Earth's glow.
const light = moon.sunDirection(time, obs);
const glow = moon.earthshine(jde);
```

## Eclipses

From the ground, the Sun and the Moon look almost the same size: the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and
about 400 times farther away. It is a stroke of luck that will not last. The Moon drifts away by about 3.8 cm a year
<Cite id="dickey1994" />, and in some 600 million years it will be too small to ever cover the Sun again. We happen to
live in the stretch of Earth's history in which the sky can go dark in the middle of the day.

With both bodies located, eclipses follow from their positions. A solar eclipse is a matter of perspective: the Moon
passing in front of the Sun, which depends on where you stand. On the line the Moon's shadow sweeps, the Sun goes out
(total), or, with the Moon near <Term of="Apogee">apogee</Term> and too small to cover it, leaves a ring of light
around the black disc (annular). A few hundred kilometers to the side, the Moon only takes a bite out of it (partial).
A lunar eclipse is the Moon passing through Earth's shadow, and it looks the same from anywhere the Moon is up: total
once the whole disc is inside the <Term of="Umbra">umbra</Term>, partial while only part of it is, and penumbral,
barely a dimming, when the Moon misses the umbra altogether (<Fig id="eclipse-views" />).

<Eclipse />
<Controls height={false} />

For a renderer, both come down to a single <Term of="Eclipse phase">phase</Term> <Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />: 0 with
the centers aligned, 0.5 where a total or annular eclipse of the Sun turns partial, 1 where the discs just touch, and
above 1 when there is nothing to see. For the Moon, 0.5 and 1 are where its center crosses the edge of the umbra and of
the penumbra. The fixed 0.5 boundary is what makes the phase usable as a texture coordinate for a shader.

Most of the time there is nothing to see, hence the four buttons (<Fig id="eclipse-views" />): they search the sky
forwards or backwards from the moment you are on and jump to the next eclipse's maximum. Nothing is looked up. NASA's
catalogs list every eclipse over five millennia <Cite id="espenak2006" /> <Cite id="espenak2009" />, but the search
finds them from the positions alone: only a full moon can enter Earth's shadow and only a new moon can cross the Sun, so
it needs to look closer only once a <Term of="Lunation">lunation</Term>. The solar search answers for the place you
chose, which is why it often lands on a modest partial eclipse.

Eclipses also come in pairs. They happen in seasons, while the Sun stands near one of the two points where the Moon's
orbit crosses the ecliptic, and a season lasts longer than the month from one new moon to the next. So every eclipse of
the Sun has one of the Moon about two weeks before or after it, if often only a faint penumbral one. The total solar
eclipse of August 12, 2026, over Greenland, Iceland and Spain, was followed by a partial lunar eclipse on August 28.

In a total lunar eclipse, the Moon darkens in the penumbra first, barely noticeable, then turns a dark red in the umbra,
lit only by sunlight that Earth's atmosphere bends around the planet, its outer rim tinged blue by the ozone the light
passes through. Tracing that light through the atmosphere gives the colors physically <Cite id="yapo2009" />, but far
from real time. A renderer can approximate them from the eclipse's `phase` instead <Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />, and
the panel only hints at them. Next to the phase, the table lists the <Term of="Eclipse magnitude">magnitude</Term>,
the number the catalogs give (<Tab id="eclipse" />).

Rare as they are, eclipses should not cost a renderer anything on every frame, yet they are special enough to be worth a
notification when one comes. This example finds the next total lunar eclipse, stepping from full moon to full moon and
narrowing each candidate down to the minute by <Term of="Bisection">bisection</Term>. The buttons above search much
the same way (<Fig id="eclipse-views" />):

```ts
import {
    eclipse, fromDate, fromJulianDay, julianDayUT, julianEphemerisDay, moon,
    toDate,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Only a full moon can be eclipsed, so step by lunations rather than
// by days. Full moons sit half a lunation after the counted new ones.
const { MEAN_NEW_MOON, MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH } = moon;
const firstFullMoon = MEAN_NEW_MOON + MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH / 2;

const MINUTE = 1 / 1440;

// The search runs in UT, the orbits in ephemeris time.
const shadow = (jd: number) => eclipse.lunar(julianEphemerisDay(jd));
const offset = (jd: number) => shadow(jd).axisOffsetKm;
// Totality: the whole disc fits inside the umbra.
const total = (jd: number) => shadow(jd).umbralMagnitude >= 1;

// The first minute between a and b where test turns true, by halving.
function bisect(a: number, b: number, test: (jd: number) => boolean) {
    while (b - a > MINUTE) {
        const mid = (a + b) / 2;
        if (test(mid)) b = mid;
        else a = mid;
    }
    return b;
}

function nextTotalLunarEclipse(from: number): Date | null {
    const gone = (from - firstFullMoon) / MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH;
    for (let month = Math.floor(gone); month < gone + 250; month++) {
        const full = firstFullMoon + month * MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH;
        // The true full moon wanders up to 14 hours from the mean one;
        // three-hour steps find the one closest to the shadow's axis.
        let [closest, km] = [full, Infinity];
        for (let jd = full - 0.75; jd <= full + 0.75; jd += 1 / 8) {
            const away = offset(jd);
            if (away < km) [closest, km] = [jd, away];
        }
        // The deepest moment: where the Moon stops closing in on the axis.
        const greatest = bisect(closest - 1 / 8, closest + 1 / 8,
            (jd) => offset(jd + MINUTE) > offset(jd));
        if (!total(greatest)) continue;
        // Totality lasts less than two hours, so it began within the hour.
        const start = bisect(greatest - 1 / 24, greatest, total);
        if (start > from) return toDate(fromJulianDay(start));
    }
    return null;
}

// Up to twenty years ahead; from late 2026 that is totality on
// 2028-12-31, from 16:18 UTC.
const next = nextTotalLunarEclipse(julianDayUT(fromDate(new Date())));
```

## A month of Moons

Put together, the pieces above make a moon calendar that shows more than the phase: which way the Moon is turned where
you are, and whether it is up while it is dark. In the calendar below (<Fig id="moon-calendar" />), the discs are the
Moon at the same time of day all month, turned as it stands in the sky there; the lanes say when it is up, and the Sun
with it. How to draw them is up to you, so the drawing falls to two functions you bring yourself:

```ts
import {
    fromDate, julianEphemerisDay, moon, sun,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const DAY_MS = 86_400_000;
const obs = { latitude, longitude };
// Upper limb on the horizon, refraction included, as for sunrise.
const isUp = (altitude: number) => altitude > -0.833;

// firstDay: the first midnight, timeOfDay: milliseconds after it.
for (let day = 0; day < days; day++) {
    const time = fromDate(new Date(firstDay + day * DAY_MS + timeOfDay));
    const jde = julianEphemerisDay(time);
    // The parallactic angle turns the sky's north into the zenith.
    const q = moon.parallacticAngle(time, obs);
    drawMoonDisc(day, {
        lit: moon.illuminatedFraction(jde),
        earthshine: moon.earthshine(jde),
        libration: moon.librations(jde),
        // Counterclockwise from up, already turned into this sky.
        brightLimb: moon.brightLimbAngle(time, obs),
        north: moon.positionAngleOfAxis(jde) - q,
        up: isUp(moon.horizontalPosition(time, obs).altitude),
    });
}

// The lanes, in ten-minute steps: the altitude picks the color.
for (let minute = 0; minute < days * 1440; minute += 10) {
    const time = fromDate(new Date(firstDay + minute * 60_000));
    drawLane("moon", minute, moon.horizontalPosition(time, obs).altitude);
    drawLane("sun", minute, sun.horizontalPosition(time, obs).altitude);
}
```

## Precise and approximate

Every quantity comes in two variants. The _precise_ one follows Jean Meeus' _Astronomical Algorithms_
<Cite id="meeus1998" />, the standard reference for this kind of computation. The _approximate_ one follows the
night sky model of Jensen et al. <Cite id="jensen2001" />, with far fewer terms. The tables show the precise values;
_approx_ in any table's heading switches all of them, and hovering an approximate value shows how far off it is.

For most rendering, the approximation is plenty. Over a year, the Sun's approximate altitude stays within 0.002° of the
precise one, invisible next to a disc half a degree wide. The Moon's can be off by almost a tenth of a degree, a fifth
of its diameter: fine for a sky dome, not for an eclipse. That is what the precise variant is for, along with the exact
moment of sunset or a telescope view. Both live behind the same names, so switching is a change of import path.

```ts
import { sun } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit"; // precise
import { sun as fast } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit/approx"; // approximate
```

Neither costs a frame anything. On a desktop CPU, a Ryzen 7 7800X3D, the precise Sun takes about 2 µs a call, the
Moon about 5 µs, and the approximate ones about 1 µs, so the quick start's two calls fit into a frame at 60 Hz some
two thousand times over. Imported alone, the Sun adds about 4 kB to a bundle, minified and compressed, the quick
start's Sun and Moon under 6 kB, and the whole library under 7 kB.

## The people behind the numbers

Almost every number in this library goes back to Jean Meeus, a Belgian meteorologist who worked at Brussels Airport
until 1993 <Cite id="rasc2003" /> and computed the sky in his spare time. His _Astronomical Algorithms_
<Cite id="meeus1998" /> turned the celestial mechanics of the observatories into recipes for a home computer, and it is
still the book astronomy libraries are checked against. The asteroid
[2213 Meeus](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/tools/sbdb_lookup.html#/?sstr=2213) carries his name <Cite id="rasc2213" />.

Þorsteinn Sæmundsson, astronomer at the University of Iceland, who computed its almanac for sixty years
<Cite id="helgadottir2023" />, fitted the refraction behind `earth.atmosphericRefraction` <Cite id="saemundsson1986" />.
The approximate variants follow the night sky model of Henrik Wann Jensen and his colleagues <Cite id="jensen2001" />,
which renders the Moon, the stars, zodiacal light and airglow together with the atmosphere they shine through.

Hendrik van de Hulst wrote the book on light scattering whose law for a sphere's phase `moon.earthshine` applies to
Earth seen from the Moon <Cite id="vandehulst1980" />. As a student in 1944, he predicted the faint 21 cm radio glow
of hydrogen between the stars, the signal radio astronomers have mapped the Milky Way with ever since
<Cite id="hovenier2001" />. And Fred
Espenak, for decades at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and known as Mr. Eclipse <Cite id="anderson2025" />, compiled
the eclipse canons with Meeus, along with the polynomials for ΔT <Cite id="espenak2006" />.
